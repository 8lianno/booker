## Purpose
This chapter flips the book's core mechanism: having shown that pleasure exacts a pain debt, it demonstrates that deliberately pressing on the pain side of the balance yields indirect, more enduring pleasure. It supplies the physiological and clinical rationale for the therapeutic self-binding and pain-seeking practices the later chapters presuppose.

## Central Question
Can intentionally inflicting moderate pain on ourselves reset the pleasure-pain balance toward pleasure — and where does that self-application of pain tip into a new addiction?

## Thesis
Because the body responds to pain with a compensatory, longer-lasting release of "pleasure" neurotransmitters, deliberately and intermittently pressing on the pain side of the balance produces enduring well-being — but pain in too potent or prolonged a form becomes its own addiction [ch08 ¶30], [ch08 ¶168–169].

## Claims
- C1. Pain produces pleasure indirectly by triggering the body's homeostatic regulation, and this pain-derived dopamine is more enduring than pleasure sought directly [ch08 ¶30–31].
- C2. Intermittent exposure to pain reweights our hedonic set point toward pleasure, making us less vulnerable to pain and more able to feel pleasure over time [ch08 ¶33].
- C3. Hormesis — moderate doses of noxious stimuli (cold, heat, radiation, fasting, exercise) — improves functioning, but the benefit reverses with too large or prolonged a dose [ch08 ¶46], [ch08 ¶48].
- C4. Applying a second painful stimulus can treat existing pain by triggering the body's own endogenous opioids [ch08 ¶70], [ch08 ¶74].
- C5. Pressing too hard or too long on the pain side becomes its own compulsive addiction, ending in a persistent dopamine-deficit state [ch08 ¶148], [ch08 ¶168].

## Evidence
- E1. Prague cold-water study — study — supports C1 — ten men submerged in 14°C water for an hour showed dopamine up 250% and norepinephrine up 530%, with dopamine staying elevated for an hour after, explaining Michael's lasting high [ch08 ¶23 "plasma (blood) dopamine concentrations increased 250 percent"] — caveat: small sample of ten men.
- E2. Solomon's shocked dogs — study — supports C2 — with repeated electric shocks the dog's terror shrank and its after-response became a "fit of joy" with deep bradycardia, showing pain morphing into pleasure through adaptation [ch08 ¶37 "wagged its tail, in what we called"] — caveat: cruel experiment not permitted today.
- E3. Sprenger heat-pain neuroimaging — study — supports C4 — a second painful stimulus lessened the felt pain of the first, and naloxone blocked the effect, implicating endogenous opioids [ch08 ¶74 "naloxone, an opioid receptor blocker, prevented this phenomenon"] — caveat: only twenty healthy young men.
- E4. Michael's escalating ice baths — case — supports C5 — Michael upped the ante with a meat freezer, ice-breaking, and a motored trough, then admitted "it sounds like an addiction," illustrating pain-seeking tipping into compulsion [ch08 ¶116 "it sounds like an addiction"] — caveat: he later reports it "never got out of hand."

## Reader Assumptions
The reader is assumed to already accept the pleasure-pain balance metaphor and its gremlins from earlier chapters, to believe pleasure carries an automatic pain cost, and to share the modern cultural reflex of avoiding pain and pursuing comfort that the chapter argues against.

## Foundational Vs Illustrative
Foundational: the homeostatic pain-to-pleasure mechanism (C1), the hedonic reweighting via intermittent pain (C2), and the addiction risk of overusing pain (C5) — these carry the book's argument forward. Illustrative: the celebrity and athlete vignettes (Wim Hof, Jimmy Kimmel, Alex Honnold, Scott Jurek, Lewis Pugh), the running-wheel research, and the historical anecdotes (Cerletti and Bini's ECT, lightning survivors) that dramatize the mechanism.

## Retain
Deliberately and intermittently pressing on the pain side of the balance recruits the body's own regulatory response to deliver longer-lasting pleasure — but the same mechanism can make pain itself addictive, so dose and duration are everything.

## Tension
The chapter's own examples undercut a clean prescription: the very cold-water immersion held up as healing recovery is the one Michael admits "sounds like an addiction," and skydivers chasing "natural highs" end up anhedonic, so the line between hormetic healing and destructive overconsumption is asserted by dose but never sharply drawn [ch08 ¶116], [ch08 ¶140].

## Recall
