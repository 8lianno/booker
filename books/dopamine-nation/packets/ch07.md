## Purpose
This chapter turns the pleasure-pain balance from a metaphor for addiction into a lens for critiquing modern psychopharmacology. Having established that self-generated overindulgence tips the balance toward pain, Lembke now asks whether medicating the pleasure side to "restore" balance is a genuine cure or a new form of the same problem. It sets up the book's pivot toward the alternative — deliberately embracing pain — that later chapters develop.

## Central Question
When a person's pleasure-pain balance is broken, can pressing on the pleasure side with medication truly restore it, or does that solution carry its own hidden costs?

## Thesis
Medications that press on the pleasure side of the balance can be lifesaving, but they risk addiction, may not work long-term, and — used to make people "biochemically indifferent to intolerable circumstance" — can medicate away essential humanity and function as social control [ch07 ¶55] [ch07 ¶78].

## Claims
- C1. Buprenorphine works by returning the pleasure-pain balance to level, letting an addicted person stop battling craving and resume living [ch07 ¶4].
- C2. Any drug that presses on the pleasure side of the balance has the potential to be addictive, even when prescribed by a doctor for a diagnosed condition [ch07 ¶56–57].
- C3. Despite rising spending on psychiatric drugs, symptom prevalence has not fallen, and some drugs may worsen the very conditions they treat over the long term [ch07 ¶58–64].
- C4. Psychotropic medication can blunt the full range of human emotion, so relief comes at the cost of feeling fully human [ch07 ¶65] [ch07 ¶68].
- C5. Psychiatric and opioid medications are prescribed disproportionately to the poor and disenfranchised, raising the worry that medication serves as social control rather than care [ch07 ¶78] [ch07 ¶85–87].

## Evidence
- E1. Chris — case — supports C1 — a Stanford student whose repeated collapses into opioid and heroin use ended when buprenorphine leveled his balance and let him finish a PhD, marry, and function; he says he never wants to stop it [ch07 ¶48 "It was like a light switch for me"] — caveat: his dependence on it is lifelong and unresolved.
- E2. David — case — supports C2 — the college student who got hooked on doctor-prescribed stimulants shows a diagnosis and prescription confer no immunity to addiction [ch07 ¶57 "have a high potential for abuse"] — caveat: single patient anecdote paired with the FDA quote.
- E3. Four-country funding data — data — supports C3 — despite large funding increases for antidepressants, anxiolytics, and hypnotics in Australia, Canada, England, and the US, mood and anxiety prevalence did not decrease from 1990 to 2015 [ch07 ¶59 "the prevalence of mood and anxiety symptoms"] — caveat: correlational; controls for poverty and trauma stated.
- E4. Olympics-commercial patient — story — supports C4 — a patient on antidepressants stopped crying at commercials and then could not cry at her mother's funeral, so she quit to feel human again [ch07 ¶68 "couldn't even cry at her own mother's funeral"] — caveat: none stated.

## Reader Assumptions
The reader is assumed to already accept the pleasure-pain balance model from earlier chapters, to know that overindulgence tips the balance toward pain, and to hold the conventional belief — promoted by pharma — that psychiatric drugs simply "replace a missing chemical" so people can function normally. The chapter works by unsettling that last assumption.

## Foundational Vs Illustrative
Foundational: the reframing that medicating the pleasure side is itself a way of pressing on the balance, and the four worries (addiction, non-efficacy, lost humanity, social control) that motivate the book's turn toward embracing pain. Illustrative: Chris's long biographical arc, David, the emotion-blunting patient stories, and the Serenity film analogy.

## Retain
Pressing on the pleasure side of the balance with medication can save lives, but it is not free: it can become addictive, may fail or backfire over time, can flatten the emotional range that makes us human, and — prescribed most heavily to the poor — risks becoming social control rather than healing.

## Tension
Lembke both prescribes buprenorphine to Chris "of course" and calls the very same treatment a possible "form of institutional neglect and even structural violence" when social needs go unaddressed — she never resolves whether the medication liberated Chris or merely managed a problem the world created [ch07 ¶88].

## Recall
- Q: Why does Lembke argue that a doctor's prescription does not make a drug safe from addiction, and what evidence does she use?
  A: Because addiction stems from pressing on the pleasure side of the balance, not from where the drug comes from — any drug that presses on that side can be addictive. She cites David, who became dependent on prescription stimulants obtained for a diagnosed condition, and notes stimulants are the molecular equivalent of street methamphetamine with, per the FDA, a "high potential for abuse" [ch07 ¶56–57].
