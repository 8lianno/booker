## Purpose
This chapter converts the earlier neuroscience of the pleasure-pain balance into a practical toolkit. Having argued that willpower alone fails against high-dopamine goods, Lembke introduces "self-binding" as the deliberate construction of barriers, and organizes it into three categories—space, time, and meaning—that later chapters presuppose when discussing recovery and moderation.

## Central Question
How can a person who cannot rely on willpower deliberately arrange their environment, schedule, and mental categories to limit compulsive overconsumption?

## Thesis
Because compulsion strips away voluntary choice in the moment, the effective remedy is self-binding—intentionally creating physical, chronological, and categorical barriers between ourselves and our drug of choice while we still can choose, so that binding ourselves becomes a way to be free [ch06 ¶17–24].

## Claims
- C1. Self-binding is the intentional, willing creation of barriers to mitigate overconsumption; it works by acknowledging will's limits rather than relying on will [ch06 ¶17].
- C2. Binding must happen before compulsion strikes, because in the throes of desire there is effectively no capacity to decide [ch06 ¶18–19].
- C3. Self-binding sorts into three categories—physical (space), chronological (time), and categorical (meaning) [ch06 ¶24].
- C4. Self-binding is fallible: barriers can become puzzles to solve, and severe addiction can defeat them through self-deception and cheating [ch06 ¶33].
- C5. Restricting consumption to a narrow window of time can moderate use and prevent the escalating consumption that unlimited access produces [ch06 ¶75].
- C6. Addictive drugs shrink our "temporal horizons," causing delay discounting that overvalues immediate rewards over long-term ones [ch06 ¶85].
- C7. Binding ourselves, though it looks like constraint, produces liberation and dignity rather than confinement [ch06 ¶169].

## Evidence
- E1. Jacob's electrical-stimulation machine — case — supports C1 — Lembke tells the relapsing patient to throw the machine somewhere unretrievable, the founding example of physical self-binding [ch06 ¶12 "put it all in the garbage"] — caveat: relapse still preceded the binding.
- E2. Oscar and the locked wine cabinet — story — supports C4 — a patient defeats his wife's locked file cabinet by uncorking the bottle with pliers and a straw, showing barriers can become challenges [ch06 ¶39 "the only remaining tool he would need: a long plastic straw"] — caveat: none stated.
- E3. Ahmed and Koob's cocaine rats — study — supports C5 — rats with six-hour access escalate to exhaustion and death, while one-hour-access rats use steady amounts [ch06 ¶74 "use steady amounts of cocaine over many consecutive days"] — caveat: animal model.
- E4. Bickel's story-completion task — study — supports C6 — opioid-addicted participants imagined a future averaging nine days versus 4.7 years for healthy controls [ch06 ¶85 "a future that was on average nine days long"] — caveat: hypothetical story prompt.

## Reader Assumptions
The reader is assumed to accept from earlier chapters that dopamine drives compulsive seeking, that willpower is a limited resource, and that modern life offers near-infinite access to high-dopamine goods—so that external rules alone cannot suffice.

## Foundational Vs Illustrative
Foundational: the definition of self-binding, its pre-compulsion timing rule, and the three-part space/time/meaning taxonomy. Illustrative: the many patient stories (Jacob, Oscar, Arnold, Emily, Muhammad, Mitch, Jasmine), the Odysseus myth, the rat and story-completion studies, and the marshmallow experiment.

## Links
prev: builds on the pleasure-pain balance and the failure of pure willpower established in the preceding chapters, turning theory into method. next: sets up later chapters on radical honesty and prosocial shame by showing that recovery requires structured external supports, not just insight.

## Retain
When willpower cannot be trusted in the moment of craving, deliberately build barriers in advance—across space, time, and meaning—because binding yourself is paradoxically a route to freedom.

## Tension
Lembke repeatedly concedes self-binding "is not fail-safe" and "too can fall prey to self-deception," yet still presents it as "a good and necessary place to start"—leaving unresolved how a method the addict can reliably cheat (Arnold hiding the pill, Oscar's straw) offers dependable protection [ch06 ¶25].

## Recall
- Q: Why does Lembke insist that self-binding be set up before a person feels the compulsion to use, and what does the cannabis patient Muhammad illustrate about this timing?
  A: Because once compulsion takes hold the reflexive pull is nearly impossible to resist and "there's no deciding," so the barrier must be built while voluntary choice remains [ch06 ¶19]. Muhammad shows the failure of ignoring this: once he smoked even one joint he was governed by the pleasure-pain balance rather than reason, and his time limits eroded from "not again till graduation" down to "no marijuana before nine a.m." [ch06 ¶117–119].
