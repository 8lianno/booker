## Purpose
This short introduction frames the entire book: it names the subject (the relationship between pleasure and pain), previews the two tools the book will combine (neuroscience of reward + lived experience of recovery), and prepares the reader to treat patient addiction stories as evidence. Every later chapter presupposes the pleasure-pain-balance premise and the dopamine-as-currency premise laid down here.

## Central Question
Why has understanding the relationship between pleasure and pain become essential to living well in an age of overwhelming abundance?

## Thesis
The book argues that in a world transformed from scarcity to compulsive abundance, finding balance requires combining the science of desire with the wisdom of recovery, because the brain processes pleasure and pain in the same place and as opposite sides of one balance [ch01 ¶8–13].

## Claims
- C1. Modern abundance has multiplied the number, variety, and potency of highly rewarding stimuli, turning ordinary consumption into a source of compulsion [ch01 ¶6].
- C2. Dopamine functions as a common measuring stick for the addictive potential of any experience: more dopamine in the reward pathway means a more addictive experience [ch01 ¶7].
- C3. The brain processes pleasure and pain in the same place and they operate as opposite sides of a single balance [ch01 ¶8].
- C4. Neuroscience alone is insufficient; the lived experience of people with addiction is needed to learn how to overcome compulsive overconsumption [ch01 ¶10–11].

## Evidence
- E1. Smartphone-as-needle framing — analogy — supports C1 — the author frames modern devices as constant dopamine delivery, illustrating that abundance itself drives compulsion [ch01 ¶6 "the modern-day hypodermic needle, delivering digital dopamine 24/7"] — caveat: rhetorical framing, not a measured claim.
- E2. Craving a second piece of chocolate — story — supports C3 — a universal everyday craving is used to show the balance tipping toward pain in the moment of wanting [ch01 ¶9 "craving a second piece of chocolate"] — caveat: illustrative example, not clinical data.
- E3. Kent Dunnington on addiction — authority — supports C4 — a philosopher/theologian is cited to justify treating addicts' stories as revealing truths about everyone [ch01 ¶11 "they show us who we truly are"] — caveat: appeal to authority, not empirical.

## Reader Assumptions
The author assumes the reader already recognizes their own hard-to-resist behaviors (sugar, shopping, social media), accepts that dopamine is linked to reward, and is willing to see extreme addiction as continuous with ordinary overconsumption rather than alien to it.

## Foundational Vs Illustrative
Foundational: the pleasure-pain balance premise, dopamine as a universal currency of addictiveness, and the plan to combine science with recovery. Illustrative: the smartphone/needle image, the chocolate craving, and the Dunnington quotation.

## Retain
Balance is the master idea: pleasure and pain share one neural seesaw, and healthy living in an age of abundance means combining the science of desire with the wisdom of recovery [ch01 ¶13].

## Tension
The introduction asserts the pleasure-pain balance as settled neuroscience while simultaneously conceding "neuroscience is not enough," leaving unresolved how much weight rests on mechanism versus on anecdotal patient wisdom [ch01 ¶10].

## Recall
- Q: Why does the author argue that neuroscience alone cannot solve compulsive overconsumption, and what does she add to it?
  A: Because understanding the brain's reward mechanism does not by itself teach people how to change behavior; the author adds the lived experience of those most vulnerable to overconsumption, people with addiction, whose recovery offers practical wisdom. The book's proposed solution combines the science of desire with the wisdom of recovery [ch01 ¶10–13]. (difficulty: application)
