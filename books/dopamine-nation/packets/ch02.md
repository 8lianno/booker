## Purpose
This chapter opens the book's diagnosis: it establishes that compulsive overconsumption is now a universal condition, not a fringe pathology, and that modern technology has made addictive rewards more accessible, potent, and various than ever. It gives the reader the vocabulary (addiction, access as risk, the dopamine/limbic economy) and the vivid case (Jacob) that every later chapter about the pleasure-pain balance presupposes.

## Central Question
Why are so many people, even those with comfortable lives, now compulsively overconsuming — and what has changed in the modern world to make this so?

## Thesis
Jacob's ever-escalating "masturbation machines" are not an isolated perversion but an extreme instance of a universal modern predicament: technology-driven abundance has multiplied the access, potency, variety, and social contagion of rewarding substances and behaviors, so that we are all now engaged with our own machines and "devouring ourselves" [ch02 ¶56], [ch02 ¶147].

## Claims
- C1. Addiction, broadly defined, is compulsive consumption of a substance or behavior despite harm — and it now afflicts even people with good, low-risk lives [ch02 ¶67], [ch02 ¶68].
- C2. Jacob's escalating machines and the author's own compulsive reading are the same phenomenon: we are all, in a sense, running our own masturbation machines [ch02 ¶56].
- C3. Easy access is among the most important risk factors for addiction; increasing supply raises exposure and harm, and decreasing supply lowers it [ch02 ¶81], [ch02 ¶84].
- C4. The "dopamine economy" / "limbic capitalism" uses technology to increase not just access but drug numbers, variety, and potency [ch02 ¶92].
- C5. The Internet drives overconsumption both by increasing access and by socially suggesting behaviors that might never otherwise have occurred to us [ch02 ¶129], [ch02 ¶130].
- C6. This compulsive overconsumption is now a global, lethal pattern threatening individuals, populations, and the planet itself [ch02 ¶141], [ch02 ¶146].

## Evidence
- E1. Jacob's masturbation machine — case — supports C2 — a patient rigs a record player, coil, and cloth to sustain preorgasm for hours, showing extreme yet self-escalating compulsion [ch02 ¶52 "the coil go up and down"] — caveat: single anecdotal patient case.
- E2. The author's romance-novel chain reading — story — supports C1 — a well-resourced professional compulsively reads erotica instead of sleeping, cooking, or attending to family, showing addiction without classic risk factors [ch02 ¶62 "reading instead of sleeping, reading instead of"] — caveat: self-reported and framed as "trivial" versus severe addiction.
- E3. Prohibition natural experiment — data — supports C3 — a nationwide alcohol ban cut public drunkenness and liver disease by half, demonstrating that reduced supply reduces addiction [ch02 ¶85 "decreased by half during this period"] — caveat: also produced a criminal black market.
- E4. Detective Dutto and the six-year-old — case — supports C5 — a child with an unsupervised iPad copies anime sex acts onto his brother, illustrating Internet-driven behavioral contagion [ch02 ¶128 "decided to try it out on his little brother"] — caveat: second-hand account via the detective, not the author's patient.

## Reader Assumptions
The author assumes the reader thinks of addiction as something that happens to other, damaged, high-risk people (trauma, poverty, "extreme perversion"), and that a comfortable, loving, successful life inoculates against compulsion. She also assumes the reader takes today's abundance of drugs, screens, food, and shopping as neutral background rather than an engineered risk.

## Foundational Vs Illustrative
Foundational: the definition of addiction, the access-as-risk principle, and the "dopamine economy"/limbic capitalism framing that later chapters build on. Illustrative: the Jacob narrative, the author's Twilight-to-Fifty-Shades arc, Max's drug timeline, Chi's shopping cycle, and the detective's anecdote, which dramatize rather than establish the mechanism.

## Retain
Compulsive overconsumption is no longer a fringe pathology of the traumatized or depraved; technology-amplified abundance (access, potency, variety, contagion) has made it a universal modern condition — Jacob's machine is a mirror, not a freak show.

## Tension
The author leans heavily on access-and-supply as "perhaps the most important risk factor" while conceding genetics, mental illness, trauma, and poverty also matter, yet she does not reconcile how a supply-driven story fits her own case — a person with no risk factors who still became compulsive [ch02 ¶89], [ch02 ¶91].

## Recall
- Q: Why does Lembke insist that Jacob's masturbation machine is relevant to ordinary readers rather than an "extreme perversion... with little or no relevance"?
  A: Because she frames the machine as a difference of degree, not kind: the same escalating pursuit of a reliable, self-adjusting reward appears in her own compulsive romance reading and in modern shopping, gaming, and porn. She argues that if we dismiss Jacob, we miss that "We are all, of a sort, engaged with our own masturbation machines," and thus miss something crucial about how we live now [ch02 ¶55], [ch02 ¶56].
