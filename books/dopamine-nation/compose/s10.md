## 10. What Readers Usually Misunderstand

The book's compressed slogans — "dopamine fast," "press on the pain side," "abstinence" — travel further than its qualifications, so the most common misreadings come from taking Lembke's metaphors and clinical shorthand literally. Each pair below states the reflexive misreading and the correction the text itself supplies.

**Misreading:** "Dopamine fasting" means literally fasting from food, or refusing all pleasure, as a kind of ascetic purge. **Correction:** The "fast" is a targeted abstinence from one's specific drug of choice — any high-dopamine substance or behavior used too much for too long — held for about a month to let the reward pathway re-level and reveal whether the drug is cause or cure of one's suffering; the diagnostic insight, not deprivation, is the point [ch05 ¶14; ch05 ¶56].

**Misreading:** The goal is to eliminate pleasure and live joylessly, since pleasure is the enemy. **Correction:** The aim is to restore a level pleasure-pain balance and its dopamine set point, not to abolish pleasure; over-consumption is condemned precisely because it resets the set point toward pain and anhedonia, so that chasing pleasure past a point yields *less* pleasure, not more. Restoring balance is what makes ordinary pleasure feel good again [ch04 ¶28; ch05 ¶56].

**Misreading:** "Pressing on the pain side" is masochism — self-punishment, or the more agony the better. **Correction:** It is calibrated hormesis: moderate, intermittent, self-limited doses of a noxious stimulus (cold, heat, fasting, exercise) that recruit the body's compensatory regulation and reweight the set point toward pleasure. Crucially the benefit reverses with too large or too prolonged a dose, and pain pursued too hard becomes its own addiction ending in a dopamine-deficit state — dose and duration are everything [ch08 ¶46; ch08 ¶168].

**Misreading:** Abstinence is a permanent, lifelong renunciation — once you quit you must never touch the thing again. **Correction:** For most patients the ~4-week fast is a *reset*, after which the majority want to return to using but to use *less*, and the final DOPAMINE step is a personalized experiment in moderation. Lembke explicitly warns moderation can backfire into escalation (the abstinence violation effect) for some, so lifelong abstinence is one outcome among several, not the default rule [ch05 ¶108; ch05 ¶114].

**Misreading:** Addiction is only about "hard" drugs — heroin, meth — and so the book is about other, damaged, high-risk people, not ordinary readers. **Correction:** Lembke defines addiction as compulsive consumption of any substance *or behavior* despite harm, afflicting even people with good, low-risk lives; Jacob's machine, her own compulsive romance-novel reading, shopping, gaming, and porn are treated as differences of degree, not kind — "we are all, of a sort, engaged with our own masturbation machines" [ch02 ¶56; ch02 ¶67].

**Misreading:** Self-binding is just willpower rebranded — you still have to "resist temptation," only harder. **Correction:** Self-binding works by *acknowledging will's limits*, not exercising them: it means building physical, chronological, and categorical barriers in advance, before compulsion strikes, because in the throes of desire "there's no deciding." It is a route to freedom precisely because it does not rely on in-the-moment self-control [ch06 ¶17; ch06 ¶18–19].

**Misreading:** Because psychiatric medications "press on the pleasure side," Lembke is anti-medication and thinks drugs like buprenorphine or antidepressants are always bad. **Correction:** She calls such medications lifesaving and prescribes buprenorphine to Chris "of course"; the argument is that pressing on the pleasure side carries hidden costs — addiction risk even under prescription, possible long-term non-efficacy, blunted emotional range, and disproportionate prescribing to the poor as a form of social control — not that medication should be abandoned [ch07 ¶4; ch07 ¶78].

**Misreading:** Shame is intrinsically toxic and should always be dissolved or avoided. **Correction:** Shame is a twin-edged emotion whose valence is set entirely by how the community responds: rejection or forced secrecy produces *destructive* shame that deepens isolation and consumption, while acceptance paired with a concrete path to amends produces *prosocial* shame that increases belonging and reduces consumption. The strict rules of groups like AA function as protective club goods, not mere punishment [ch10 ¶8; ch10 ¶77].

**Misreading:** "Radical honesty" is confessional oversharing — dumping every dark thought performatively counts as the discipline. **Correction:** Lembke distinguishes truth-telling that exposes real vulnerabilities and carries consequences (which builds awareness, intimacy, and an accountable autobiography) from "disclosure porn" / the "drunkalogue," a curated performance of confession that does the opposite work. The lever is owning small and large truths honestly, not spectacle [ch09 ¶28; ch09 ¶80].

**Misreading:** The whole framework is settled, mechanistic neuroscience — the "balance" is a literal organ and the numbers are physiological constants. **Correction:** Lembke states outright that neuroscience "is not enough" and that the balance is only a metaphor: pleasure and pain are more complex, can occur simultaneously, and not everyone starts level. Even the load-bearing four-week figure is a rough clinical average with wide individual variation, not a fixed constant — the model rests as much on recovery wisdom and clinical observation as on brain imaging [ch04 ¶69; ch01 ¶10].
