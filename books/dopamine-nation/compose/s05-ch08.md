### Chapter 7 — Pressing on the Pain Side
_Covers [ch08 ¶1–169]_

**Thesis** — Because the body answers pain with a compensatory, longer-lasting release of "pleasure" neurotransmitters, deliberately and intermittently pressing on the pain side of the balance produces enduring well-being — but pain in too potent or prolonged a form becomes its own addiction [ch08 ¶30; ch08 ¶168–169].

**Why it matters** — This chapter is the hinge on which the book's therapeutic program turns. Having spent earlier chapters proving that pleasure exacts an automatic pain debt, Lembke now runs the same homeostatic machinery in reverse: pain, applied on purpose, buys back pleasure. The claim converts the pleasure-pain balance from a warning ("indulgence costs you") into a prescription ("discomfort pays you"), and it supplies the physiological and clinical rationale for the self-binding and pain-seeking practices the rest of the book presupposes. It also reframes the modern reflex to avoid all discomfort as itself a cause of the anhedonia the book diagnoses.

**What it adds** — It builds directly on the pleasure-pain balance and the pain-after-pleasure debt established in Chapter 3 (ch04), now inverting that dynamic so the after-response falls on the pleasure side rather than the pain side. Forward, it sets up the therapeutic use of intentional pain and self-binding that the following chapters develop into concrete prescriptions for patients — making this the mechanistic bridge between the book's diagnosis and its treatment half.

**Core concepts** — *Pressing on the pain side* [ch08 ¶30]: intentionally choosing pain to recruit the body's regulatory rebound. *Hormesis* [ch08 ¶46]: moderate doses of noxious stimuli (cold, heat, radiation, fasting, exercise) improve functioning, while too large or prolonged a dose reverses the benefit. *Pain to treat pain* / heroic therapies [ch08 ¶70]: applying a second painful stimulus to relieve existing pain via endogenous opioids. The chapter also refines the *hedonic set point* [ch08 ¶33] and the *dopamine-deficit state* [ch08 ¶148] introduced earlier, showing how intermittent pain reweights the former and how overdoing pain lands one in the latter.

**Key evidence** —
- Prague cold-water study: ten men submerged in 14°C water for an hour showed dopamine up 250% and norepinephrine up 530%, dopamine staying elevated an hour afterward, explaining the lasting high [ch08 ¶23 "plasma (blood) dopamine concentrations increased 250 percent"]; small sample of ten men.
- Sprenger heat-pain neuroimaging: a second painful stimulus lessened the felt pain of the first, and naloxone blocked the effect, implicating the body's own opioids [ch08 ¶74 "naloxone, an opioid receptor blocker, prevented this phenomenon"]; only twenty healthy young men.
- Michael's escalating ice baths: he upped the ante with a meat freezer, ice-breaking, and a motored trough, then conceded "it sounds like an addiction," showing pain-seeking tipping into compulsion [ch08 ¶116 "it sounds like an addiction"]; he later reports it "never got out of hand."

**Retain** — Deliberately and intermittently pressing on the pain side of the balance recruits the body's own regulatory response to deliver longer-lasting pleasure — but the same mechanism can make pain itself addictive, so dose and duration are everything. The chapter's own cases blur its clean line: the healing cold immersion is also the one that "sounds like an addiction," and pain chased too hard ends in the dopamine-deficit state the treatment was meant to escape [ch08 ¶116; ch08 ¶148].
