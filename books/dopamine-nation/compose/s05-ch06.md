### Chapter 5 — Space, Time, and Meaning
_Covers [ch06 ¶1–169]_

**Thesis** — Because compulsion strips away voluntary choice in the moment of craving, the effective remedy is not more willpower but self-binding: intentionally erecting physical, chronological, and categorical barriers between ourselves and our drug of choice while we still can choose, so that binding ourselves paradoxically becomes a way to be free [ch06 ¶17–24].

**Why it matters** — This is the book's pivot from diagnosis to treatment. The preceding chapters argued that dopamine drives compulsive seeking and that willpower is a limited resource that reliably fails against near-infinite access to high-dopamine goods. If that is true, exhortations to "try harder" are useless. Chapter 5 answers the resulting practical question directly: how does a person who cannot trust their in-the-moment judgment still limit overconsumption? The answer—design the environment, the schedule, and the mental categories in advance—reframes constraint as an act of agency rather than defeat, and gives the reader something to actually do.

**What it adds** — In the book's build-up this chapter turns theory into method. It builds on the pleasure-pain balance and the demonstrated failure of pure willpower established in the preceding chapters, and it sets up the later chapters on radical honesty and prosocial shame by showing that recovery depends on structured external supports, not on insight alone (packet Links). It is the hinge: everything before it explains why we overconsume; everything after it presupposes that self-binding, not sheer resolve, is the mechanism of change.

**Core concepts** — Self-binding, the intentional and willing creation of barriers that works by acknowledging will's limits rather than relying on will [ch06 ¶17]; the pre-compulsion timing rule, since binding must be set up before desire strikes when there is effectively no capacity to decide [ch06 ¶18–19]; and the three-part taxonomy—physical self-binding (space), chronological self-binding (time), and categorical self-binding (meaning) [ch06 ¶24]. Two supporting mechanisms explain why time-binding works: delay discounting [ch06 ¶79] and shrunken temporal horizons [ch06 ¶85], whereby addictive drugs make us overvalue immediate rewards over long-term ones. Crucially, self-binding is presented as fallible: barriers can become puzzles to solve, and severe addiction can defeat them through self-deception and cheating [ch06 ¶33].

**Key evidence** —
- Ahmed and Koob's cocaine rats: animals given six-hour access escalate to exhaustion and death, while one-hour-access rats "use steady amounts of cocaine over many consecutive days" [ch06 ¶74], showing that restricting consumption to a narrow window moderates use (a study; caveat: animal model).
- Oscar and the locked wine cabinet: a patient defeats his wife's locked file cabinet by uncorking the bottle with pliers and "the only remaining tool he would need: a long plastic straw" [ch06 ¶39], demonstrating that barriers can become challenges the addict simply solves (a story supporting the fallibility claim).
- Bickel's story-completion task: opioid-addicted participants imagined "a future that was on average nine days long" [ch06 ¶85] versus 4.7 years for healthy controls, evidence for shrunken temporal horizons and delay discounting (a study; caveat: hypothetical story prompt).

**Retain** — When willpower cannot be trusted in the moment of craving, deliberately build barriers in advance—across space, time, and meaning—because binding yourself is paradoxically a route to freedom rather than confinement [ch06 ¶169].
