### Introduction — The Problem
_Covers [ch01 ¶1–13]_

**Thesis** — In a world remade from scarcity into compulsive abundance, finding balance requires fusing the science of desire with the wisdom of recovery, because the brain processes pleasure and pain in the same place and as opposite sides of a single balance [ch01 ¶8–13]. This is the load-bearing premise every later chapter presupposes: the mechanism claim (one shared neural site) and the method claim (neuroscience plus lived recovery) are stated together at the outset so the rest of the book can treat both as given.

**Why it matters** — The introduction supplies the two premises that make the book coherent. First, the pleasure-pain balance metaphor: pleasure and pain are not separate systems but one seesaw, so pursuing pleasure inevitably tips toward pain. Second, dopamine as a universal measuring stick: the more dopamine a stimulus releases in the reward pathway, the more addictive it is [ch01 ¶7]. Without these two premises, the DOPAMINE-acronym self-help program and the patient case studies that fill later chapters would have no unifying frame. The introduction also licenses a specific reading protocol — treating named addiction stories as evidence about everyone, not just the clinically ill.

**What it adds** — As the opening (prev: none), this section establishes the problem before any mechanism or remedy is detailed. Per the packet Links field, it sets up Chapter 1's deeper account of "masturbation machines" (highly rewarding modern stimuli) and the later mechanistic treatment of the pleasure-pain balance and dopamine fasting. It is pure scaffolding: it names the subject (the relationship between pleasure and pain), previews the two tools the book combines (reward neuroscience plus recovery experience), and primes the reader to see extreme addiction as continuous with ordinary overconsumption.

**Core concepts** — The pleasure-pain balance, introduced as the master metaphor [ch01 ¶8]; dopamine reframed as the currency of addictive potential [ch01 ¶7]; and compulsive overconsumption, the behavior the whole book aims to address [ch01 ¶12]. These three are the seeds of the book's terminology system, each expanded mechanistically in later chapters.

**Key evidence** —
- The smartphone-as-needle analogy, framing modern devices as constant dopamine delivery to show that abundance itself drives compulsion [ch01 ¶6 "the modern-day hypodermic needle, delivering digital dopamine 24/7"]. Rhetorical framing, not a measured claim.
- The everyday craving for a second piece of chocolate, used to show the balance tipping toward pain in the moment of wanting [ch01 ¶9 "craving a second piece of chocolate"]. Illustrative, not clinical data.
- Kent Dunnington cited to justify treating addicts' stories as revealing truths about everyone [ch01 ¶11 "they show us who we truly are"]. Appeal to authority, not empirical.

**Retain** — Balance is the master idea: pleasure and pain share one neural seesaw, and healthy living in an age of abundance means combining the science of desire with the wisdom of recovery [ch01 ¶13]. Note the built-in tension the reader should carry forward: the introduction asserts the pleasure-pain balance as settled neuroscience yet concedes "neuroscience is not enough," leaving unresolved how much weight rests on mechanism versus anecdotal patient wisdom [ch01 ¶10].
