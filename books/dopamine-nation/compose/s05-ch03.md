### Chapter 2 — Running from Pain
_Covers [ch03 ¶1–93]_

**Thesis** — Our compulsive flight from every form of discomfort — through pills, distraction, and a culture that pathologizes ordinary suffering — is precisely what is deepening our suffering [ch03 ¶85] [ch03 ¶92–93]. The chapter states the paradox bluntly at its close: "the reason we're so miserable may be that we're working so hard to avoid being miserable" [ch03 ¶93]. Avoidance is not a side effect of the problem; it is the pathology.

**Why it matters** — This is the chapter that names the disease the rest of the book treats. Chapter 1 catalogued the overwhelming supply of high-dopamine drugs and devices; here Lembke turns to the demand side and the cultural motive — an organized, society-wide effort to eliminate pain. It supplies the motivating paradox (more wealth, freedom, and medical progress, yet more misery) that the later neuroscience of the pleasure-pain balance is meant to explain and that the self-binding chapters are meant to remedy. Without this chapter the reader would have the mechanism but not the reason anyone should care.

**What it adds** — It builds directly on Chapter 1's account of ubiquitous access to high-dopamine goods by showing the flip side: not just chasing pleasure but actively fleeing discomfort. It then sets up Chapter 3's pleasure-pain balance, the neuroscientific mechanism that will explain why avoiding pain backfires physiologically. Positioned second, it converts a description of abundance into a diagnosis of misery, closing the "why unhappy amid plenty" gap that the neuroscience chapters then answer mechanistically.

**Core concepts** — "running from pain" as the organizing frame for modern avoidance [ch03 ¶85]; "poisons become vitamins," the loop in which drugs taken to compensate for self-neglect get reclassified as medical necessities [ch03 ¶73]; Rieff's "psychological man born to be pleased" as the cultural type produced by a happiness-maximizing ethic [ch03 ¶25]; the overpathologized, oversanitized childhood that leaves children intolerant of ordinary adversity [ch03 ¶35]; and boredom reframed not as a deficit but as opportunity [ch03 ¶62].

**Key evidence** — (1) David — a high-functioning software developer who escalates to Adderall, Ambien, and Ativan, reframing self-inflicted depletion from fifteen-hour workdays and heavy running as ADHD and anxiety until he is hospitalized [ch03 ¶73 "poisons become vitamins"]; the case grounds C4's compensation-then-blame loop. (2) Psychiatric-drug prevalence — more than one in four American adults "takes a psychiatric drug on a daily basis" amid a global rise in prescribing [ch03 ¶46], supporting the claim that medicine has shifted toward eliminating all pain. (3) World Happiness Report and pain surveys — Americans reported being less happy in 2018 than 2008, and "Thirty-four percent of Americans said they felt pain" [ch03 ¶91], more than any other country surveyed — the correlational backbone of the inverse relationship between comfort and well-being.

**Retain** — Insulating ourselves from all pain does not produce happiness; it produces more pain — the avoidance itself is the pathology [ch03 ¶93]. Note the chapter's own hedge: Lembke grants that empathy-informed parenting and safe spaces are "a positive evolution" yet blames the same trends for fragility, without fixing where healthy protection ends and harmful oversanitizing begins [ch03 ¶34].
