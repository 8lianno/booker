### Chapter 1 — Our Masturbation Machines
_Covers [ch02 ¶1–147]_

**Thesis** — Jacob's ever-escalating "masturbation machines" are not an isolated perversion but an extreme instance of a universal modern predicament: technology-driven abundance has multiplied the access, potency, variety, and social contagion of rewarding substances and behaviors, so that we are all now "engaged with our own masturbation machines" [ch02 ¶56] and "devouring ourselves" [ch02 ¶147]. Jacob's rig is a mirror held up to the reader, not a freak show to be dismissed.

**Why it matters** — This is the book's opening diagnosis, and everything downstream depends on the reader accepting it. If compulsive overconsumption is a fringe pathology of the traumatized and depraved, the pleasure-pain neuroscience that follows is a niche clinical curiosity. By insisting instead that addiction, broadly defined as "compulsive consumption of a substance or behavior despite harm," now afflicts even people with good, low-risk lives [ch02 ¶67; ch02 ¶68], Lembke makes the whole book personally addressed to the comfortable, successful reader who assumed they were exempt.

**What it adds** — It converts the Introduction's abstract claim — that a book on pleasure and pain is timely for an age of indulgence — into a concrete, vivid picture of what overconsumption actually looks like, and supplies the working vocabulary (addiction, access-as-risk, the dopamine economy) the rest of the book presupposes. Per the packet's Links field, it sets up the following chapters' neuroscience of the pleasure-pain balance: this chapter names the problem and its modern engineering; the next explains the brain mechanics that make it inevitable.

**Core concepts** — *Addiction* redefined broadly [ch02 ¶67]; the *double life* of the seemingly high-functioning consumer [ch02 ¶42]; the *dopamine economy* / *limbic capitalism*, in which technology is used to increase not just access but drug numbers, variety, and potency [ch02 ¶92]; *polypharmacy* [ch02 ¶100]; *social contagion*, whereby the Internet suggests behaviors that might never otherwise have occurred to us [ch02 ¶129; ch02 ¶130]; and *deaths of despair* [ch02 ¶145]. The load-bearing principle uniting them is that easy access is "among the most important risk factors" — increasing supply raises exposure and harm, decreasing it lowers them [ch02 ¶81; ch02 ¶84].

**Key evidence** —
- Jacob's masturbation machine: a patient rigs a record player, coil, and cloth to sustain preorgasm for hours, showing extreme yet self-escalating compulsion [ch02 ¶52 "the coil go up and down"]. Single anecdotal patient case, but the book's anchoring image.
- The author's own romance-novel chain reading: a well-resourced professional compulsively reads erotica, "reading instead of sleeping, reading instead of" attending to family [ch02 ¶62], demonstrating addiction absent classic risk factors. Self-reported and framed as "trivial" beside severe addiction.
- The Prohibition natural experiment: a nationwide alcohol ban cut public drunkenness and liver disease, which "decreased by half during this period" [ch02 ¶85], evidence that reduced supply reduces addiction — though it also spawned a criminal black market.

**Retain** — Compulsive overconsumption is no longer a fringe pathology of the traumatized or depraved; technology-amplified abundance (access, potency, variety, contagion) has made it a universal, now global and lethal, modern condition [ch02 ¶141; ch02 ¶146] — Jacob's machine is a mirror, not a freak show.
