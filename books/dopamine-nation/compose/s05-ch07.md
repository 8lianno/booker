### Chapter 6 — A Broken Balance?

_Covers [ch07 ¶1–92]_

**Thesis** — Medications that press on the pleasure side of the balance can be lifesaving, but they carry hidden costs: they risk addiction, may not work over the long term, and — when used to make people "biochemically indifferent to intolerable circumstance" — can medicate away essential humanity and function as social control [ch07 ¶55] [ch07 ¶78]. The chapter converts the pleasure-pain balance from a metaphor for self-inflicted addiction into a lens for interrogating modern psychopharmacology itself.

**Why it matters** — This is the hinge where Lembke's own profession comes under scrutiny. Having spent earlier chapters showing that overindulgence tips the balance toward pain, she now asks the harder question: when a person's balance is already broken, does medicating the pleasure side truly restore it, or does it install a new version of the same problem? By treating prescribed drugs — buprenorphine, antidepressants, stimulants — as chemically continuous with the illicit indulgence she has been describing, she dissolves the reader's comfortable assumption that a doctor's prescription simply "replaces a missing chemical" and is therefore safe. The stakes are both clinical and ethical: relief can come at the price of feeling fully human, and the pattern of who gets medicated raises the question of whether psychiatry heals or manages.

**What it adds** — Structurally this chapter is the pivot. It builds directly on the pleasure-pain balance and homeostasis of the earlier chapters (especially the balance metaphor of ch04 and the dopamine-depletion findings), redirecting those mechanisms from street indulgence onto prescribed medicine. Its forward function is to motivate the book's turn: it previews explicitly that "there is an alternative path that might work better: embracing pain," which the following chapters — pressing on the pain side — develop. The four worries raised here (addiction, non-efficacy, lost humanity, social control) are the negative case that makes the positive alternative necessary.

**Core concepts** — the pleasure-pain balance, refined from ch04 and reapplied to prescribed drugs [ch07 ¶4]; buprenorphine (Suboxone) as a drug that returns the balance to level [ch07 ¶4]; opioid-induced hyperalgesia, where opioids increase sensitivity to pain [ch07 ¶61]; tardive dysphoria, drug-induced worsening of the treated condition over time [ch07 ¶64]; and clinical abandonment / structural violence as the ethical frame for who is medicated [ch07 ¶88].

**Key evidence** —
- Chris, a Stanford student whose repeated collapses into opioid and heroin use ended when buprenorphine leveled his balance, letting him finish a PhD, marry, and function — he never wants to stop it [ch07 ¶48 "It was like a light switch for me"]. Supports the claim that pressing on the pleasure side can be genuinely lifesaving; caveat: his dependence is lifelong and unresolved.
- David, a college student who got hooked on doctor-prescribed stimulants, showing that a diagnosis and prescription confer no immunity to addiction — stimulants being the molecular equivalent of street methamphetamine, with, per the FDA, a "high potential for abuse" [ch07 ¶57 "have a high potential for abuse"]. Supports the claim that any drug pressing on the pleasure side can be addictive.
- Four-country funding data: despite large funding increases for antidepressants, anxiolytics, and hypnotics in Australia, Canada, England, and the US, "the prevalence of mood and anxiety symptoms" did not decrease from 1990 to 2015 [ch07 ¶59 "the prevalence of mood and anxiety symptoms"]. Supports the non-efficacy worry; caveat: correlational.

**Retain** — Pressing on the pleasure side of the balance with medication can save lives, but it is not free: it can become addictive, may fail or backfire over time (opioid-induced hyperalgesia, tardive dysphoria), can flatten the emotional range that makes us human — the patient who, numbed by antidepressants, "couldn't even cry at her own mother's funeral" [ch07 ¶68] — and, prescribed most heavily to the poor and disenfranchised, risks becoming social control rather than healing. Lembke never fully resolves the tension her own case exposes: she prescribes buprenorphine to Chris "of course," yet calls that same treatment a possible form of "institutional neglect and even structural violence" when the social conditions driving the suffering go unaddressed [ch07 ¶88].
