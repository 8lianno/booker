## 12. Internal Tensions And Limits

These tensions are internal to the book — places where its own claims, concessions, and examples pull against each other. Each is drawn from a chapter's own text; none imports outside critique. The pattern is consistent: Lembke builds a clean mechanistic or prescriptive model, then concedes complications that the model does not absorb, and the two are left standing side by side.

**1. Mechanism as settled science vs. "neuroscience is not enough."** The book's foundational move is to present the pleasure-pain balance and opponent-process neuroadaptation as established fact: dopamine indexes addictive potential, and any tip toward pleasure triggers an equal, opposite tip toward pain [ch04 ¶10; ch04 ¶16–20]. Yet the introduction simultaneously concedes that neuroscience alone cannot carry the argument, leaning instead on patient stories and clinical wisdom for its real persuasive weight [ch01 ¶10]. The tension is never resolved: the reader is asked to accept the balance as literal biology and, at the same time, to trust anecdote precisely where the biology runs out. How much of the case rests on mechanism versus on accumulated first-person cases is left indeterminate.

**2. The balance as literal mechanism vs. "The Balance Is Only a Metaphor."** For most of Chapter 3 the fulcrum is treated as if it were a real, level teeter-totter that tips and self-corrects by a fixed rule [ch04 ¶16–20]. Then, in a section titled to that effect, Lembke concedes that "in real life, pleasure and pain are more complex," that the two can occur simultaneously (spicy food), and that not everyone starts level [ch04 ¶69; ch04 ¶71]. The concession undercuts the tidy model the chapter spent its length constructing: if pleasure and pain are co-located and simultaneous rather than opposed and sequential, the opponent-process image is a simplification doing rhetorical work it cannot fully cash out.

**3. Supply-and-access as "perhaps the most important risk factor" vs. her own unexplained case.** Lembke elevates easy access to among the most important risk factors for addiction, arguing that increasing supply raises harm and decreasing it lowers harm [ch02 ¶81; ch02 ¶84], and generalizes this into "limbic capitalism" driving global overconsumption. But she also presents herself and low-risk patients like Jacob as people with none of the standard risk factors — no genetics, trauma, or poverty — who nonetheless became compulsive [ch02 ¶89; ch02 ¶91]. A supply-driven story explains exposure but not why an otherwise-protected person tips into compulsion; the book does not reconcile the population-level supply thesis with the individual case that motivates it.

**4. Self-binding as reliable protection vs. the addict who can always cheat it.** Self-binding is offered as the core practical remedy — a way to mitigate overconsumption by building barriers before desire strikes [ch06 ¶17]. Lembke openly concedes it "is not fail-safe" and "too can fall prey to self-deception," yet still calls it "a good and necessary place to start" [ch06 ¶25]. Her own illustrations show the barriers failing (Arnold hiding the pill; patients treating restrictions as puzzles to solve) [ch06 ¶33]. The method is thus recommended precisely for the severe cases in which the book admits it is least dependable, with no criterion given for when a barrier will hold.

**5. Medication as liberation vs. medication as "structural violence."** In Chapter 6 Lembke prescribes buprenorphine "of course" and frames it as returning the balance to level so an addicted person can resume living [ch07 ¶4]. In the same chapter she quotes approvingly that the identical treatment, offered without addressing patients' social needs, "can turn into a form of institutional neglect and even structural violence" [ch07 ¶88], and notes such drugs go disproportionately to the poor and disenfranchised [ch07 ¶85–87]. Whether the medication liberated Chris or merely managed a problem the surrounding world created is left open — the book endorses the pharmacological fix and indicts it as social control without deciding between them.

**6. Hormetic pain as healing vs. pain-seeking as its own addiction.** The prescription to "press on the pain side" holds up cold-water immersion and comparable stressors as therapeutic, arguing intermittent pain reweights the set point toward pleasure [ch08 ¶33]. Yet the book's own witnesses collapse the distinction: the cold-immersion enthusiast Michael admits his practice "sounds like an addiction" [ch08 ¶116], and skydivers chasing a "natural high" end up anhedonic [ch08 ¶141]. The line between hormetic healing and destructive overconsumption is asserted by dose — moderate helps, too much reverses [ch08 ¶46; ch08 ¶48] — but never sharply drawn, so the same behavior reads as cure or disease depending on which anecdote is cited.

**Limits of the evidence base.** Beyond these paired tensions, the book's warrant is often thinner than its confidence. Its most mechanistic bridge — that practicing honesty strengthens the prefrontal cortex the way piano practice builds circuits — is admitted to be unproven; the cited experiment shows only that stimulating the cortex increases honesty, not the reverse, and the researcher offers "no definitive answer" [ch09 ¶43]. The four-week fast is presented as both the usual minimum and usually sufficient, then immediately qualified by wide individual variation and a roughly 20 percent non-response rate, with the key supporting study left open to several competing explanations [ch05 ¶62; ch05 ¶63; ch05 ¶67]. And the closing prescription — escape the pain of pursuing pleasure by immersing ourselves in life — rests on two patient anecdotes and the author's own story rather than demonstration [ch11 ¶20]. Across the book, clean numbers and vivid cases carry claims that the underlying data support only loosely, and the reader should treat the crisp models as heuristics the author herself repeatedly softens.
