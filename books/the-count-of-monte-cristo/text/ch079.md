[¶1] LXXIX LEMONADE
[¶2] Morrel was indeed very contented. M. Noirtier had just sent for him, and he was in such a hurry to know why that he had not taken a cab, trusting his own two legs more than those of a hired horse. He had set out at a fair pace from the Rue Meslay and was on his way to the Faubourg Saint-Honoré. He was proceeding at a jog, while poor Barrois followed on as best he could. Morrel was thirty-one, Barrois sixty; Morrel was drunk with love, Barrois faint with heat. The two men, so different in age and interests, were like two sides of a triangle: separated at the base, meeting at the apex; the apex was Noirtier.
[¶3] He had told Morrel to make haste, and Morrel, much to the despair of Barrois, was following this instruction precisely. When they arrived, Morrel was not even out of breath – love gives wings – but Barrois, who had not been in love for a long time, was pouring with sweat.
[¶4] The old servant showed Morrel in by the side entrance and closed the study door. Soon the sound of a dress brushing against the floor announced the arrival of Valentine. She was ravishingly beautiful in her mourning clothes. The dream was becoming so sweet that Morrel would almost have done without talking to Noirtier; but the old man’s wheelchair could soon be heard outside, and he came in.
[¶5] With a benevolent smile Noirtier accepted the thanks which Morrel heaped upon him for the miraculous intervention that had saved Valentine and himself from despair. Then Morrel’s look turned towards the young woman, enquiring of her the reason why he was newly in favour. She, shyly sitting at some distance from Morrel, was waiting to speak until she was obliged to. Noirtier looked at her in turn.
[¶6] ‘Must I say what you told me to, then?’ she asked.
[¶7] ‘Yes,’ Noirtier indicated.
[¶8] ‘Monsieur Morrel,’ Valentine said, addressing the young man who was devouring her with his eyes. ‘My grandpapa Noirtier had a thousand things to tell you, and he has told them to me in the past three days. Today, he has sent for you so that I can repeat them. I shall do so, since he has chosen me as his interpreter, without changing a word of his meaning.’
[¶9] ‘I am impatient to hear,’ the young man replied. ‘Please speak, Mademoiselle, speak.’
[¶10] Valentine lowered her eyes. This was a sign that seemed to Morrel to augur well: Valentine was weak only when she was happy.
[¶11] ‘My grandfather wants to leave this house,’ she said. ‘Barrois is trying to find him suitable lodgings.’
[¶12] ‘What about you, Mademoiselle,’ said Morrel, ‘you, who are so dear and so essential to Monsieur Noirtier?’
[¶13] ‘I shall not abandon my grandfather,’ the young woman said. ‘This has been agreed between us. My apartment will be near his. Either I shall have Monsieur de Villefort’s consent to go and live with grandpapa Noirtier, or it will be refused. In the first case, I shall leave at once; in the second, I shall await my majority, which falls in eighteen months. Then I shall be free, I shall have independent means and…’
[¶14] ‘And?’ said Morrel.
[¶15] ‘And, with my grandfather’s permission, I shall keep the promise that I made to you.’
[¶16] Valentine spoke these last words so softly that Morrel would have been unable to hear them, had they not meant as much to him as they did.
[¶17] ‘Isn’t that your opinion, grandpa?’ she added, turning to Noirtier.
[¶18] ‘Yes,’ said the old man.
[¶19] ‘Once I am settled in at my grandfather’s,’ Valentine added, ‘Monsieur Morrel will be able to come and visit me in the presence of this good and worthy protector. Our hearts may be ignorant or capricious; but if the bond that they have started to form seems respectable and gives some guarantees of future happiness to our endeavour – though, alas, they do say that hearts which are fired to overcome obstacles go cold when these are removed – then Monsieur Morrel can ask me for my hand; I shall wait for him.’
[¶20] ‘Oh!’ cried Morrel, tempted to kneel in front of the old man as before God, and in front of Valentine as before an angel. ‘Oh! What have I done in my life to deserve such happiness?’
[¶21] ‘Until then,’ the young woman continued, in her clear, strict voice, ‘we shall respect the conventions, and even the will of our parents, provided that will does not attempt to separate us for ever. In a word, and I repeat it, because it says everything, we shall wait.’
[¶22] ‘I swear to carry out the sacrifices that the word imposes, Monsieur,’ Morrel said, ‘not with resignation, but with joy.’
[¶23] ‘So,’ Valentine went on, with a look that was very dear to Maximilien’s heart, ‘no more rash deeds, my friend. Do not compromise one who, from today onwards, considers herself destined to bear your name with purity and dignity.’
[¶24] Morrel put his hand to his heart.
[¶25] During this time, Noirtier was watching them both tenderly. Barrois, who had stayed at the back of the room, as someone from whom they had nothing to hide, was smiling and wiping the huge beads of sweat that coursed across his bald head.
[¶26] ‘Heavens above, look how hot he is, poor Barrois,’ said Valentine.
[¶27] ‘That’s because I had a good run, Mademoiselle,’ Barrois said. ‘But I must grant him this, Monsieur Morrel ran even faster than I did.’
[¶28] Noirtier’s eyes turned towards a tray on which were a carafe of lemonade and a glass. Half an hour earlier, Noirtier himself had drunk what was missing from the jug.
[¶29] ‘Go on, my dear Barrois,’ the girl said. ‘Have it: I can see that you are casting envious looks at that half-empty jug.’
[¶30] ‘The fact is,’ he replied, ‘I am dying of thirst and I should dearly like to drink a glass of lemonade to your health.’
[¶31] ‘Go on, then,’ said Valentine. ‘Come back in a moment.’
[¶32] Barrois took the tray and no sooner was he in the corridor than they saw him, through the door that he had forgotten to close, toss back his head to empty the glass that Valentine had filled.

[¶34] ‘It is midday,’ she said. ‘And as today is Saturday, grandpapa, this must be the doctor.’
[¶35] Noirtier made a sign confirming that he agreed with her.
[¶36] ‘Since he is going to come here, Monsieur Morrel must leave, mustn’t he, grandpapa?’
[¶37] ‘Yes,’ the old man replied.
[¶38] ‘Barrois!’ Valentine called. ‘Barrois, come here!’

[¶40] ‘Barrois will take you to the door,’ Valentine said to Morrel. ‘Now, Captain, remember one thing, which is that my grandfather advises you not to do anything which might threaten out future happiness.’
[¶41] ‘I promised to wait,’ said Morrel, ‘and I shall.’

[¶43] ‘Who rang?’ Valentine asked.
[¶44] ‘Doctor d’Avrigny,’ Barrois said, staggering.

[¶46] ‘He’s going to fall!’ Morrel cried.
[¶47] The trembling in Barrois’ limbs was increasing at an alarming rate and his expression was contorted by the contractions of the muscles, suggesting a violent nervous seizure.
