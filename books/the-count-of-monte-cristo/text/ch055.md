[¶1] LV MAJOR CAVALCANTI
[¶2] Neither the count nor Baptistin had lied when they told Morcerf that the major from Lucca was to visit, which was Monte Cristo’s excuse for refusing the invitation to dinner.
[¶3] The clock had just struck seven – M. Bertuccio, as instructed, having left for Auteuil at two o’clock – when a cab drew up at the door, then hurried off, in what looked like shame, immediately after depositing at the gate a man of around fifty-two, wearing one of those green frock-coats, frogged in black, which seem in Europe to belong to an undying breed of garment. Wide blue trousers; boots still clean, although their polish was questionable and their sole a trifle too thick; suede gloves; a hat, in shape close to a gendarme’s; and a black collar edged in white which, if its owner were not wearing it by choice, might have been mistaken for an iron yoke: such was the picturesque costume worn by the person who rang the outer bell, asking if it was not here, at number 30, Avenue des Champs-Elysées, that the Count of Monte Cristo lived; and who, on receiving the answer ‘Yes’, closed the gate behind him and walked towards the front steps.
[¶4] The man’s small, angular head, his greying hair and his thick, grey moustache identified him to Baptistin, who had a precise description of the visitor and was waiting for him in the hall. So, no sooner had he announced his name to the intelligent servant than Monte Cristo was informed of his arrival. The stranger was introduced into the simplest drawing-room, where the count was waiting and came to greet him with a welcoming smile.
[¶5] ‘My dear sir,’ he said. ‘Welcome. I was expecting you.’
[¶6] ‘Really?’ said the Luccan. ‘Your Excellency was expecting me?’
[¶7] ‘Yes, I was informed that you would be arriving this evening at seven.’
[¶8] ‘You were informed of my arrival?’
[¶9] ‘Precisely.’
[¶10] ‘I’m so glad. I must admit, I was afraid that they had forgotten to take that little precaution.’
[¶11] ‘Which one?’
[¶12] ‘That of informing you in advance.’
[¶13] ‘No, not at all.’
[¶14] ‘But are you sure you are not mistaken?’
[¶15] ‘Quite sure.’
[¶16] ‘I am really the person that Your Excellency was expecting today at seven?’
[¶17] ‘Yes, you are. In any case, we can make sure.’
[¶18] ‘Oh, if you were expecting me, there is no need,’ said the Luccan.
[¶19] ‘Yes, there is!’ said Monte Cristo. The man looked faintly uneasy.
[¶20] ‘Now, let’s see,’ said Monte Cristo. ‘Aren’t you the Marquis Bartolomeo Cavalcanti?’
[¶21] ‘Bartolomeo Cavalcanti,’ the Luccan repeated, joyfully. ‘That’s right.’
[¶22] ‘Formerly a major in the service of the Austrian army?’
[¶23] ‘Was I a major?’ the old soldier asked timidly.
[¶24] ‘Yes, a major,’ said Monte Cristo. ‘That is the name we give in France to the rank that you held in Italy.’
[¶25] ‘Very well, I ask nothing better, you understand.’
[¶26] ‘In any event,’ said Monte Cristo, ‘you have not come here on your own initiative.’
[¶27] ‘No, no, certainly not.’
[¶28] ‘You were sent by someone.’
[¶29] ‘Yes.’

[¶31] ‘That’s right!’ the major exclaimed delightedly.
[¶32] ‘Do you have a letter?’
[¶33] ‘Here it is.’
[¶34] ‘So, you see! Give it to me.’ And Monte Cristo took the letter, opened it and read it. The major watched him, his eyes wide with astonishment, then looked curiously at every part of the room, though always eventually turning back to its owner.
[¶35] ‘Here we are… the dear abbé… “Major Cavalcanti, a worthy physician of Lucca, descendant of the Cavalcantis of Florence,” ’ Monte Cristo said, reading, ‘ “… who possesses a fortune of half a million in income.” ’ The count looked up from the paper and bowed. ‘ “Of half a million…” I say! My dear Monsieur Cavalcanti…’
[¶36] ‘Does it say half a million?’ the Luccan asked.
[¶37] ‘In so many words. And it must be true, because Abbé Busoni is the man who knows most about the great fortunes of Europe.’
[¶38] ‘Let it be half a million, then,’ said the Luccan. ‘But, on my word, I didn’t think it was such a sum.’
[¶39] ‘That’s because you have a steward who robs you. What do you expect, dear Monsieur Cavalcanti, we must all go through it.’
[¶40] ‘You have just shown me the light,’ the Luccan said gravely. ‘I shall dismiss him.’
[¶41] Monte Cristo continued reading: ‘ “… and who only needs one thing for his happiness…” ’
[¶42] ‘Yes, my God! Yes, one thing,’ said the Luccan with a sigh.
[¶43] ‘ “… To find the son he adores.” ’
[¶44] ‘The son he adores!’
[¶45] ‘ “… who was abducted in his youth either by an enemy of his noble family or by gypsies.” ’
[¶46] ‘At the age of five, Monsieur,’ said the Luccan, sighing deeply and raising his eyes towards heaven.
[¶47] ‘Poor father!’ said Monte Cristo; then continued: ‘ “I am giving him hope, I am restoring him to life, Monsieur le Comte, by telling him that you may be able to find this son, whom he has sought in vain for fifteen years.” ’
[¶48] The Luccan looked at Monte Cristo with an indefinable expression of anxiety.
[¶49] ‘I can,’ said Monte Cristo.
[¶50] The major drew himself up to his full height. ‘So!’ he said. ‘So! The letter was true then from beginning to end?’
[¶51] ‘Did you ever doubt it, my dear Monsieur Bartolomeo?’
[¶52] ‘No, never. How could it be! A serious man like Abbé Busoni, a man with that aura of sanctity, could not allow himself to joke on such a matter. But you have not read everything, Excellency.’
[¶53] ‘True! There is a postscript.’
[¶54] ‘Yes,’ the Luccan repeated. ‘There is… a postscript.’
[¶55] ‘ “To spare Major Cavalcanti the trouble of having to transfer funds to his banker, I am sending an order of two hundred thousand francs for his travelling expenses and a credit on you in the sum of forty-eight thousand francs which you still owe me.” ’
[¶56] The major followed this postscript anxiously.
[¶57] ‘Good,’ the count said.
[¶58] ‘He said “good”,’ the Luccan muttered. ‘So, Monsieur…’
[¶59] ‘So?’ Monte Cristo asked.
[¶60] ‘The postscript…’
[¶61] ‘What about the postscript?’
[¶62] ‘You welcome it as favourably as the rest of the letter?’
[¶63] ‘Certainly. We have an arrangement, Abbé Busoni and I. I don’t know if it is exactly forty-eight thousand livres that I owe him, but we are not going to quarrel about a few banknotes. Come, come! Did you attach such importance to that postscript, my dear Monsieur Cavalcanti?’
[¶64] ‘I must confess,’ the Luccan replied, ‘that, full of confidence in Abbé Busoni’s signature, I did not take any other funds with me so that, if this letter had failed, I should have been greatly embarrassed here in Paris.’
[¶65] ‘Is a man like yourself ever embarrassed anywhere?’ said Monte Cristo. ‘Come now!’
[¶66] ‘Heavens, not knowing anyone!’ said the Luccan.
[¶67] ‘But you are known…’
[¶68] ‘Yes, I am known, so that…’
[¶69] ‘Carry on, Monsieur Cavalcanti!’
[¶70] ‘So that you will give me the forty-eight thousand livres?’
[¶71] ‘As soon as you request it.’
[¶72] The major’s eyes opened wide in astonishment.
[¶73] ‘But sit down,’ said Monte Cristo. ‘I really don’t know what can have come over me. I have kept you standing for a quarter of an hour.’
[¶74] ‘Please don’t mention it.’ The major drew up a chair and sat down.
[¶75] ‘Now,’ said the count, ‘would you like something to drink: a glass of sherry, port or alicante?’
[¶76] ‘Alicante, since you offer it. It is my favourite wine.’
[¶77] ‘I have some excellent alicante. With a biscuit, perhaps?’
[¶78] ‘With a biscuit, since you insist.’
[¶79] Monte Cristo rang and Baptistin appeared. The count went over to him and whispered: ‘Well?’
[¶80] ‘The young man is there,’ the valet answered, in the same manner.
[¶81] ‘Good. Have you brought him in?’
[¶82] ‘In the blue drawing-room, as Your Excellency ordered.’
[¶83] ‘Perfect. Bring some alicante and biscuits.’ Baptistin went out.
[¶84] ‘I really am embarrassed at the trouble I am giving you.’
[¶85] ‘Oh, come now!’ said Monte Cristo. Baptistin returned with the glasses, the wine and the biscuits.
[¶86] The count filled one glass and, into the second, poured only a few drops of the ruby liquid from the bottle, which was covered in cobwebs and all the other signs that indicate the age of a wine more surely than wrinkles do that of a man. The major followed the pouring out of the wine, and took the full glass and a biscuit.
[¶87] The count ordered Baptistin to put the tray within reach of his guest’s hand and the Luccan began by taking a sip of the alicante, giving a look of satisfaction, then gently dipping the biscuit into the glass.
[¶88] ‘So, Monsieur,’ Monte Cristo said, ‘you live in Lucca, you are rich, you are noble, you enjoy universal respect: you have everything that might make a man happy.’
[¶89] ‘Everything, Excellency,’ the major said, devouring his biscuit. ‘Absolutely everything.’
[¶90] ‘There was only one thing needed to complete your happiness?’
[¶91] ‘Only one thing.’
[¶92] ‘Which was to recover your child?’
[¶93] ‘Ah!’ said the major, taking another biscuit. ‘But it was a desperate need.’ He looked up and tried to sigh.
[¶94] ‘Now, then, dear Monsieur Cavalcanti,’ Monte Cristo said. ‘Who was this much-loved son? Because I have been told that you remained a bachelor.’
[¶95] ‘So people thought, Monsieur,’ said the major. ‘And I myself…’
[¶96] ‘Yes,’ Monte Cristo continued. ‘You gave credence to that belief. A youthful error that you wanted to hide from everyone.’
[¶97] The Luccan drew himself up and adopted the calmest and most dignified air that he could, at the same time modestly lowering his eyes, either to keep up appearances or to assist his imagination, all the while looking from under his eyebrows at the count, the fixed smile on whose lips expressed the same unfailing, benevolent curiosity.
[¶98] ‘Yes, Monsieur,’ he said. ‘I wanted to hide my error from everyone.’
[¶99] ‘Not for your sake,’ said Monte Cristo. ‘A man is above such things.’
[¶100] ‘Oh, no, certainly, not for my sake,’ the major said with a smile and a shake of the head.
[¶101] ‘But for the child’s mother,’ said the count.
[¶102] ‘For his mother!’ the Luccan exclaimed, taking a third biscuit. ‘For his poor mother!’
[¶103] ‘Have another glass, dear Monsieur Cavalcanti,’ said Monte Cristo, pouring him some more alicante. ‘The emotion is stifling you.’
[¶104] ‘For his poor mother!’ the Luccan muttered, trying to find out whether an effort of will might not act upon the lachrymal duct and dampen the corner of his eye with a false tear.
[¶105] ‘Who belonged to one of the leading families in Italy, I believe?’
[¶106] ‘A patrician lady from Fiesole, Monsieur le Comte; a patrician from Fiesole.’
[¶107] ‘Whose name was?’
[¶108] ‘You want to know her name?’

[¶113] ‘A marchesa?’

[¶122] ‘I think so,’ said the Luccan.
