## Purpose
Supplies the management principle that makes the hybrid organization of the previous chapter actually workable: dual reporting, in which one employee answers simultaneously to a mission-oriented supervisor and a functional (technical) supervisor. It extends the idea to peer-group supervision and the two-plane organization, and closes by naming cultural values as the mode of control the next chapter takes up. Later chapters presuppose that readers accept ambiguity in reporting lines and the culture/trust prerequisite established here.

## Central Question
Can an employee have two bosses — and how must supervision be split so that hybrid organizations get both business responsiveness and technical competence at once?

## Thesis
Hybrid organizations can function only through dual reporting: each person is supervised both by a mission-oriented manager who sets operating priorities and by a functional supervisor — an individual or a peer group — who sets technical standards; the arrangement is ambiguous and unloved, but nothing simpler works [ch11 ¶10] [ch11 ¶15–16].

## Claims
- C1. Dual reporting is born whenever no single boss can supply both the functional standards a job requires and the day-to-day monitoring of how it is performed, as with security staff at outlying plants [ch11 ¶6–8].
- C2. Because promotion inevitably puts managers over specialists whose fields they know nothing about, and because routing all specialists to functional seniors would destroy the general manager's ability to coordinate toward a single business purpose, both kinds of supervision must coexist — which is dual reporting [ch11 ¶9–10].
- C3. The technical supervisor need not be one person: a council of peers can exercise that supervision, but only if members voluntarily surrender individual decision-making to the group, which depends on trust — an aspect of corporate culture, not of organizational structure [ch11 ¶11–14].
- C4. Dual reporting makes a manager's life ambiguous, yet no simpler alternative works: a strictly functional organization cuts engineering and manufacturing off from the marketplace, while a purely mission-oriented one fragments and performs poorly [ch11 ¶15–16].
- C5. The hybrid form and its dual reporting are an inevitable consequence of large-organization membership in every domain — companies, universities, whatever — and complexity cannot be escaped by playing with reporting arrangements, though needless bureaucracy should be mercilessly slashed [ch11 ¶23–25].
- C6. In the two-plane (or multi-plane) organization a person occupies separate hierarchies on separate charts at once — multiplying a know-how manager's leverage, letting a senior manager serve as a "foot soldier" where appropriate, and enabling the transitory teams a fast-changing business must rely on [ch11 ¶29–36].

## Evidence
- E1. Plant security dilemma — story — supports C1 — Intel's staff can't decide whether outlying-plant guards should report to the ignorant local plant manager or the absent corporate expert; the answer that "security personnel should report jointly" to both solves the standards problem and the monitoring problem at once [ch11 ¶7 "security personnel should report jointly"] — caveat: Grove admits the staff accepted it only tentatively and the birth was slow and laborious.
- E2. Vacation-with-another-couple analogy — analogy — supports C3 — joining a peer council is likened to two couples vacationing together: you give up doing exactly what you want because "you'll have more fun, even while you'll have less freedom", showing why surrendering individual decision-making can be rational [ch11 ¶12 "you'll have more fun, even while you'll have less freedom"] — caveat: an analogy about fun, not a demonstration that peer decisions are sound; that rests on trust.
- E3. Ohio University planning council — case — supports C5 — a Wall Street Journal story shows a university forming a faculty–administration council to allocate scarce funds, with a student-affairs member saying "But I made a big pitch for buying a new bulldozer", evidence that peer bodies teach institutional thinking outside business too [ch11 ¶24 "But I made a big pitch for buying a new bulldozer"] — caveat: single press anecdote, with Grove's own bracketed glosses mapping it onto his categories.
- E4. Cindy the process engineer — case — supports C6 — Cindy spends 80 percent of her time in a crisp plant hierarchy but also sits on an inter-plant process coordinating group on a second chart, making such groups "a way for managers, especially know-how managers, to increase their leverage" from one plant to all plants [ch11 ¶32 "especially know-how managers, to increase their leverage"] — caveat: none stated.

## Reader Assumptions
That the reader carries in the functional vs. mission-oriented distinction and the argument that all large organizations end up hybrid (previous chapter), knows recurring characters like the Breakfast Factory and Cindy the know-how manager, holds the conventional belief that "a person has to have a boss" — the intuition the chapter works to loosen — and already accepts leverage as the measure of managerial contribution.

## Foundational Vs Illustrative
Foundational: the dual reporting principle itself; supervision by a peer group and its precondition of voluntarily surrendered decision-making resting on trust and culture; the two-plane organization; transitory teams as the response to rapid change. Illustrative: NASA's matrix management, the plant security story, the controller and advertising examples, the Ohio University council, Cindy's coordinating group, the vacation-couples, church-membership, and Army Reserve analogies.

## Retain
A person can — and in a hybrid organization must — have two bosses: a mission-oriented supervisor for operating priorities and a functional supervisor (person or peer group) for technical standards; this dual reporting works only inside a strong, trusted corporate culture, and no simpler arrangement actually works.

## Tension
Grove concedes the system breeds an ambiguity "most people don't like" and defends it purely by elimination — "We have tried everything else" — likening it to democracy: not good in itself, merely the least-bad option, an argument resting on Intel's experience rather than demonstration [ch11 ¶15–16]. There is also an unresolved rub between his order to "mercilessly slash away unnecessary bureaucratic hindrance" and a chapter that keeps adding councils, coordinating bodies, and planes, with only "the test of common sense" offered to tell necessary coordination from busywork [ch11 ¶25].

## Recall
- Q: In the plant security story, why was reporting solely to the corporate security manager rejected, and what did each boss contribute under the joint arrangement?
  A: The security manager sits at headquarters, so he could never know whether guards at an outlying plant even showed up, came in late, or performed badly. Under dual reporting he specifies how the job ought to be done — the standards side — while the local plant manager monitors how it is performed day by day [ch11 ¶7]. (difficulty: recall)
- Q: A company sets up a council of divisional manufacturing managers to give each other technical supervision, but members keep acting unilaterally whenever the group's decision inconveniences them. What does Grove say is missing, and why can't an org-chart fix supply it?
  A: The council only works if members voluntarily surrender individual decision-making and go along with peer decisions in most instances. That surrender depends on trusting the soundness of the group's actions, and Grove insists trust is not an organizational principle at all but an aspect of corporate culture — shared values, beliefs, and ways of doing things — so a strong positive culture is the prerequisite no reporting arrangement can substitute for [ch11 ¶12–14]. (difficulty: application)
