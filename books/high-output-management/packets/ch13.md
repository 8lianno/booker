## Purpose
This chapter pivots the book from systems (production, leverage, meetings, organization) to individuals. Since a manager's output is the output of his organization, everything covered so far is useless unless team members continually offer their best; the rest of the book is about the means to elicit peak individual performance [ch13 ¶3–4]. It introduces the training/motivation split and the motivation half of that pair, which later chapters on task-relevant maturity, performance appraisal, compensation, and training presuppose.

## Central Question
How can a manager elicit peak performance from subordinates when motivation cannot be imposed from outside but must come from within?

## Thesis
A manager cannot motivate anyone directly; he can only create an environment in which self-motivated people flourish, and the highest-leverage environment is one where work takes on the characteristics of competitive sports, because self-actualization — unlike every lower need — never extinguishes itself and drives performance without limit [ch13 ¶8, ¶30, ¶49–51].

## Claims
- C1. When a person is not doing his job there are only two possible reasons — he is not capable or not motivated — and the test is whether he could do the work if his life depended on it; accordingly a manager has exactly two levers on performance: training and motivation [ch13 ¶5–6].
- C2. Motivation has to come from within somebody, so all a manager can do is create an environment in which motivated people can flourish; and motivation is measured by performance, not by professed attitude or feeling [ch13 ¶8–9].
- C3. Following Maslow, needs create drives that produce motivation, and a need once satisfied stops motivating — so sustaining high motivation requires keeping some needs unsatisfied at all times [ch13 ¶12–13].
- C4. The lower needs (physiological, safety/security, social) can get people to show up for work, but only esteem/recognition and self-actualization make them perform once there [ch13 ¶25].
- C5. Self-actualization — the need to achieve one's utter personal best, whether competence-driven or achievement-driven — is the only source of motivation that does not extinguish itself when gratified; it motivates to ever higher performance without limit [ch13 ¶30–31].
- C6. Money motivates only until lower-level needs are satisfied; at the self-actualization level it becomes a measure of achievement rather than a utility, and as a measure it motivates without limit — which is why the raise test (absolute sum vs. comparison with others) reveals where a person sits in the hierarchy [ch13 ¶37–39].
- C7. The way to lead people toward self-actualization is to endow work with the characteristics of competitive sports — rules of the game, indicators, a "racetrack" to run on — with the manager in the role of the coach: credit-free, tough, and a former player [ch13 ¶49–51] [ch13 ¶54].

## Evidence
- E1. Intel facilities maintenance "building czar" program — case — supports C7 — years of mediocre building upkeep resisted pressure and inducement, but the moment each building's condition was scored and compared, all buildings improved dramatically with no money or rewards added: "What they did get was a racetrack, an arena of competition" [ch13 ¶51 "What they did get was a racetrack, an arena of competition"] — caveat: a single internal example; Grove generalizes from one program.
- E2. Caribbean assembly plant workers — case — supports C6 — employees enjoying a standard of living well above the local population worked only until they reached a predetermined sum, then quit, showing money stops motivating once the felt need is met [ch13 ¶37 "many employees worked just long enough to accumulate some small sum"] — caveat: from the plant's early years; contrasted with the venture capitalist for whom money measures achievement [ch13 ¶38 "money as a measure of achievement will motivate without limit"].
- E3. Ring-toss psychology experiment — study — supports C5 — uninstructed volunteers split into gamblers, conservatives, and achievers; the achievers walked just far enough away that ringing a peg was a challenge, working at the boundary of their capability and demonstrating that "some people simply must test themselves" [ch13 ¶33 "some people simply must test themselves"] — caveat: unnamed study, no citation given; also reused to show punishment breeds conservatism [ch13 ¶43].
- E4. The Hungarian Olympic fencing champion — story — supports C5 — after emigrating he quit fencing because U.S. competition was too weak to give him a contest; with no possibility of improvement the competence-driven feedback loop died and "he felt his skill was diminishing" every time he fenced [ch13 ¶40 "he felt his skill was diminishing"] — caveat: personal acquaintance anecdote, not systematic evidence.

## Reader Assumptions
Grove assumes the reader has accepted the earlier key sentence that a manager's output is the output of the organization under his supervision or influence [ch13 ¶3]. He assumes most readers think motivating means "doing something to another person" [ch13 ¶8], that they share the cultural prejudice that sports are fun while long hours of work mark a "workaholic" [ch13 ¶50], and that they manage knowledge workers for whom fear-based motivation no longer works as it did for manual laborers [ch13 ¶11]. No prior knowledge of Maslow is assumed — the hierarchy is explained from scratch [ch13 ¶12–14].

## Foundational Vs Illustrative
Foundational: the capable/motivated dichotomy and the training–motivation pair [ch13 ¶5–6]; motivation as internal, environment as the manager's only lever [ch13 ¶8]; the Maslow hierarchy and the unsatisfied-need principle [ch13 ¶12–13]; self-actualization as limitless [ch13 ¶30]; money as measure [ch13 ¶38]; task-relevant feedback [ch13 ¶41]; the sports/coach model [ch13 ¶51, ¶54]. Illustrative: the homemaker returning to work and Jim the young engineer (social needs) [ch13 ¶21–22]; Chuck at Harvard Business School and the earthquake (movement up and down the hierarchy) [ch13 ¶23–24]; the vice-president's mid-life crisis [ch13 ¶28]; the skateboarding teenager and virtuoso violinist [ch13 ¶31]; Joe Frazier [ch13 ¶48]; the newspaper columnist after the merger [ch13 ¶52].

## Retain
You cannot motivate anyone; you can only build an environment — measures, indicators, competition, a racetrack — in which self-actualization takes over, because that is the one need that never extinguishes itself and therefore drives performance without limit [ch13 ¶8, ¶30, ¶51].

## Tension
Grove dismisses fear-based motivation as an obsolete relic of galley slaves and Dickens' time [ch13 ¶10–11], yet concedes fear persists in the upper modes as fear of failure, which "can be either" a positive or negative source of motivation [ch13 ¶43]. The sports analogy sharpens rather than resolves this: competition motivates the facilities crew, but the shocked ring-tosser shows that punishing failure turns achievers conservative, and the ideal coach must be "tough on his team" [ch13 ¶54] while somehow avoiding the preoccupation with failure that Grove says destroys the self-actualized state [ch13 ¶44, ¶53]. Where toughness ends and paralyzing fear begins is left unspecified.

## Recall
- Q: A subordinate gets a raise and cares only about how it compares with what his peers received. According to Grove, what does this reveal about him, and why?
  A: He is motivated by esteem/recognition or self-actualization, not by physiological or safety needs. In the lower modes money matters for its absolute sum because it buys necessities and security; in the upper modes money is no longer a utility but a measure of achievement, so only its relative size carries information [ch13 ¶39]. (difficulty: application)
- Q: Why did the Intel facilities maintenance group's performance improve dramatically when nothing material changed?
  A: The building-czar scoring program gave the group a racetrack — an arena of competition with rules and indicators. No money or rewards were added; having your building receive the top score became the motivator, illustrating that endowing work with the characteristics of competitive sports elicits peak performance [ch13 ¶51].
