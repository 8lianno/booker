## Purpose
Applies the book's people-management machinery to two emotionally charged edge cases that fall outside routine appraisal and delegation: interviewing a prospective employee and rescuing a valued employee who announces he is quitting. It extends the appraisal chapter's judgment problem to strangers (where the manager has almost no data) and shows the one-on-one/output logic operating under time pressure and emotional load.

## Central Question
How should a manager handle the two hardest interpersonal tasks — judging a stranger's future performance in an hour, and keeping a valued subordinate who has decided to leave?

## Thesis
Both tasks are high-risk and cannot be delegated or avoided: interviewing is a nearly impossible judgment of potential that careful technique can only improve, not guarantee, and a valued employee's resignation is a managerial failure that must be countered immediately and pursued on behalf of the company, not just the department [ch16 ¶3; ch16 ¶7; ch16 ¶29–33].

## Claims
- C1. Interviewing is even harder than performance appraisal — assessing a stranger's likely performance in a new environment in about an hour — yet the manager has no choice but to do it, accepting a high risk of failure [ch16 ¶7].
- C2. The interview is the manager's to control: the applicant should do 80 percent of the talking on subjects familiar to both parties, and the interviewer must interrupt and redirect because interview time is his only asset [ch16 ¶10–11].
- C3. Interview questions should yield four categories of information: the candidate's technical/skill knowledge, what he did with what he knew, the reasons for discrepancies between capability and performance, and his operational values [ch16 ¶14].
- C4. Hiring forces the manager to judge potential contribution — explicitly at odds with the "potential trap" principle from performance reviews — and neither direct questions, hypotheticals, nor reference checks make the judgment reliable [ch16 ¶20–21].
- C5. When a valued employee says "I quit," the initial reaction is crucial: he is quitting because he feels unimportant, so the manager must drop everything, let him talk, and buy time rather than argue or defer [ch16 ¶30–31].
- C6. The manager owes it to the company to save the employee for the firm even by giving him to another department; reciprocal corporate citizenship means all managers win in the long run [ch16 ¶33].
- C7. The stakes exceed one person: other superior performers identify with the valued employee and will track his fate, so their morale and commitment hinge on the outcome [ch16 ¶37].

## Evidence
- E1. Harvard MBA wafer-cost exercise — case — supports C3 — Grove walks a food-industry candidate through semiconductor manufacturing and asks him to derive a wafer's finished cost; the candidate reasons his way to the correct answer, and the hire succeeds because the hypothetical revealed what he could do with knowledge rather than what he already knew [ch16 ¶22 "his problem-solving capacity was first-rate"] — caveat: a single anecdote; Grove elsewhere denies any technique is foolproof.
- E2. The annual-report candidate — story — supports C4 — a prospective manager arrives with Grove's annual report marked up with penetrating questions Grove cannot answer; Grove is impressed, hires him, and the hire fails, proving that even strong interview signals do not predict performance [ch16 ¶23 "We hired him and he failed badly on the job"] — caveat: none stated.
- E3. Grove's carefully interviewed disaster — story — supports C1 — for a high-level Intel position Grove interviews as thoroughly as he can, hires, and gets a from-day-one failure; reviewing his notes years later he still cannot see what he missed, so care only shifts the odds [ch16 ¶27 "it merely increases your odds of getting lucky"] — caveat: Grove offers it precisely to deny guarantees, not to discredit careful interviewing.
- E4. The hallway "Do you have a minute?" scene — story — supports C5 — the resignation is delivered while the manager rushes to a meeting; escaping to the meeting confirms the employee's feeling of unimportance and makes the outcome inevitable, which is why the first response decides everything [ch16 ¶30 "quitting because he feels he is not important"] — caveat: Grove restricts the scenario to dedicated employees, not those leaving for money or perks.

## Reader Assumptions
The reader is a manager who has absorbed the earlier material on performance appraisal (including the warning against assessing "potential"), accepts that a subordinate's output is the manager's output, and already conducts hiring interviews and retention conversations as part of the job. Grove also assumes the reader operates inside a multi-department company where internal transfer is a live option.

## Foundational Vs Illustrative
Foundational: the four information categories [ch16 ¶14], the admission that hiring requires judging potential despite the appraisal principle [ch16 ¶20], the drop-everything protocol for a resignation [ch16 ¶31], and the duty to save the employee for the company [ch16 ¶33]. Illustrative: the list of managers' favorite questions [ch16 ¶13], the wafer-cost and annual-report anecdotes [ch16 ¶22–23], the Rickover three-legged-chair trick Grove rejects in favor of straightforwardness [ch16 ¶26], and the scripted dialogue defusing the "blackmailer" worry [ch16 ¶34–36].

## Links

## Retain
Both tasks are unavoidable and high-risk: interview to extract the four categories of information while controlling your only asset — time — and when a valued employee quits, react instantly, save him for the company even at your own department's expense, because every other top performer is watching.

## Tension
Grove concedes an internal contradiction: hiring requires judging "potential contribution," the very thing his performance-review chapter warned managers to avoid, and he resolves it only by calling the task "tricky and high-risk, but unfortunately unavoidable" [ch16 ¶20]. He then undercuts his own methods further: after his most careful interview produced a day-one disaster, he admits careful interviewing guarantees nothing and merely improves your odds of getting lucky [ch16 ¶27].

## Recall
- Q: A candidate answers every question impressively and asks penetrating questions of his own. According to Grove, how much confidence should this give you, and why?
  A: Limited confidence. Grove was deeply impressed by a candidate who arrived with a marked-up annual report full of questions Grove could not answer — and the man failed badly on the job [ch16 ¶23]. Even Grove's most careful interview and reference work produced a from-day-one disaster he still cannot explain, so interviewing merely increases your odds of getting lucky rather than guaranteeing success [ch16 ¶27]. (difficulty: application)
- Q: Why does Grove insist you must handle a resignation at the very first mention rather than after your important meeting?
  A: Because a dedicated employee quits when he feels he is not important to you. Mumbling about talking later confirms exactly that feeling and makes the outcome inevitable, whereas dropping what you are doing conveys by action that he matters; the first conversation is only for listening and buying time, not for winning him back [ch16 ¶30–31]. (difficulty: recall)
