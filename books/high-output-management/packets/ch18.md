## Purpose
This is the book's final substantive chapter, and it converts the whole argument into a closing managerial duty: since a manager's output is his organization's output, and training is one of only two levers for raising subordinate performance, the manager must personally teach. It gathers earlier threads — output-centered management, leverage, and role modeling — into one concrete practice, complete with a step-by-step program for a first course.

## Central Question
Who should train employees — outside specialists or the manager himself — and how should a manager actually go about doing it?

## Thesis
Because insufficiently trained employees quietly destroy output, and because training (alongside motivation) is one of the two ways a manager can raise subordinate performance, training is among the highest-leverage activities a manager can perform — and it cannot be delegated to specialists, since effective training must mirror actual organizational practice, run as a continuing process, and be delivered by a believable, practicing role model: the boss [ch18 ¶5–9, ¶13–14].

## Claims
- C1. Insufficiently trained employees, despite their best intentions, produce inefficiencies, excess costs, unhappy customers, and sometimes dangerous situations — costs that make training's importance obvious to any manager who runs into them [ch18 ¶5].
- C2. A manager's output is the output of his organization, so his two levers for raising individual subordinate performance are motivation and capability — and since motivating employees is accepted as undelegatable, the same logic should apply to training [ch18 ¶7–8].
- C3. Training is one of the highest-leverage activities a manager can perform: twelve hours of course preparation and lecturing that yield even a 1 percent performance improvement across ten subordinates return about two hundred hours of work [ch18 ¶9].
- C4. For training to be effective it must be closely tied to how things are actually done in the organization — canned outside courses fail this test — and it must be a reliable, scheduled, continuing process rather than a one-time rescue event [ch18 ¶10–12].
- C5. The manager himself must do the teaching, because the instructor must be a believable, practicing authority and suitable role model; proxies, however well versed, cannot fill that role [ch18 ¶13–14].
- C6. Organizations face two training tasks of very different magnitude — teaching new members job skills versus teaching new ideas or skills to present members — and the second can be roughly five times as large and enormously costly, so it should not be entered into lightly [ch18 ¶18–20].
- C7. Teaching a task demands much deeper knowledge than doing it, which is why the teacher himself learns the most from developing a course [ch18 ¶29].

## Evidence
- E1. Restaurant with the lost liquor license — story — supports C1 — an untrained reservations clerk never tells callers to bring their own wine, so the maitre d' apologizes to every party and no one has wine, "all because one employee was not properly trained" [ch18 ¶3 "because one employee was not properly trained"] — caveat: Grove admits he doesn't know for sure that no one instructed her.
- E2. Ion implanter incident at Intel — case — supports C1 — a new operator untrained to spot an out-of-tune machine ran nearly a day's wafers under wrong conditions; "material worth more than one million dollars" was scrapped and customer deliveries slipped [ch18 ¶4 "material worth more than one million dollars"] — caveat: single dramatic instance from Grove's own company.
- E3. The four-lecture leverage arithmetic — thought-experiment — supports C3 — twelve hours of preparation for ten students who will work twenty thousand hours next year yields, at a 1 percent improvement, "the equivalent of two hundred hours of work" [ch18 ¶9 "the equivalent of two hundred hours of work"] — caveat: the 1 percent gain is assumed, not measured, and depends on training addressing real job needs (¶10).
- E4. Outside consultants' career-development course at Intel — case — supports C4 — consultants taught structured career plans and coordinated rotations while Intel's actual tradition was a free market of internal openings; troubled by the disparity, "the participants got a bit demoralized" [ch18 ¶11 "the participants got a bit demoralized"] — caveat: one course; the failure is attributed to mismatch with practice, not to outsiders per se.
- E5. Intel's manager-taught university — data — supports C5 — Intel runs a catalogue of over fifty classes, spends "2 percent to 4 percent of our employees' time" in classroom learning with much instruction given by its own managerial staff, and Grove himself teaches performance reviews, meetings, and a company introduction [ch18 ¶15 "4 percent of our employees’ time is spent in classroom"] — caveat: evidence that manager-led training is practiced at scale, not proof it outperforms alternatives.

## Reader Assumptions
Grove assumes the reader is an already overscheduled manager who accepts that training matters but instinctively believes delivering it is someone else's job — a training specialist's. He assumes the reader has internalized the book's core doctrine that a manager's output is his organization's output and thinks in terms of leverage, and that the reader has enough command of his subordinates' work to be a credible instructor, needing only a practical push (make a list, inventory teachers, start with one short course) to begin.

## Foundational Vs Illustrative
Foundational: the two-lever model of subordinate performance [ch18 ¶8]; the leverage calculation making training a highest-leverage activity [ch18 ¶9]; the effectiveness conditions — tied to practice, process not event [ch18 ¶10, ¶12]; the role-model argument that the manager must teach [ch18 ¶13–14]; the new-employee vs. new-skill distinction [ch18 ¶18–19]. Illustrative: the restaurant story [ch18 ¶3]; the ion implanter loss [ch18 ¶4]; the demoralizing consultants' course [ch18 ¶11]; Intel's university catalogue and Grove's own teaching repertoire [ch18 ¶16–17]; the how-to program for a first course, including the throwaway first delivery and anonymous critiques [ch18 ¶21–27]; the stick-shift-over-the-phone challenge [ch18 ¶29].

## Retain
Training subordinates is not delegatable overhead but one of the highest-leverage things a manager can do — and it only works if the boss himself teaches it, tied to real practice, as a scheduled ongoing process.

## Tension
Grove insists that proxies "no matter how well versed they might be in the subject matter" cannot replace the manager as teacher [ch18 ¶14], yet a dozen paragraphs later he advises that if your organization needs many repetitions of the course, you should "set yourself up to train a few instructors" [ch18 ¶26] — reintroducing exactly the proxies the role-model argument ruled out, with no account of why trained delegates are believable where specialists were not. Relatedly, the leverage math of ¶9 rests on an assumed 1 percent improvement, which Grove immediately concedes holds only if training addresses what students actually need [ch18 ¶10] — an assumption the demoralizing consultants' course shows can fail.

## Recall
- Q: Grove concedes managers are overscheduled and that training specialists exist — so on what grounds does he argue the manager must nevertheless do the training personally?
  A: Three grounds. First, by parity with motivation: a manager's output is his organization's output, motivation and training are his two levers on subordinate performance, and no one thinks motivating can be delegated [ch18 ¶7–8]. Second, effective training must match how things are actually done and run as a continuing process, which insiders are positioned to deliver [ch18 ¶10–13]. Third, the teacher must be a believable, practicing authority and role model, a part proxies cannot play [ch18 ¶14]. (difficulty: recall)
- Q: A manager objects that preparing four lectures is twelve hours she cannot spare. Using Grove's arithmetic, how would he answer, and what condition must hold for the answer to work?
  A: Grove would compute the leverage: ten students will work about twenty thousand hours next year, so a mere 1 percent performance improvement returns the equivalent of two hundred hours for twelve invested [ch18 ¶9]. The condition is that the training accurately addresses what students need to do their jobs better and stays closely tied to actual organizational practice — otherwise, like the outside career-development course, it can even demoralize [ch18 ¶10–11]. (difficulty: application)
