## Purpose
This chapter applies the book's motivation and performance-appraisal machinery to money: it shows how salary, bonuses, and promotions can be turned into task-relevant feedback rather than mere reward. It presupposes the Maslow-based motivation discussion and the performance-rating vocabulary from earlier chapters, and it closes the loop between appraisal and consequences. Later material on training and the reader's own managerial practice presuppose that performance is visibly valued through pay and promotion decisions.

## Central Question
How should a manager dispense money — bonuses, base salary, and promotions — so that it delivers task-relevant feedback and drives performance, given that a middle manager's output cannot be measured precisely?

## Thesis
Managers should deliberately structure compensation and promotion so that they signal performance: bonuses scaled to compensation level, merit-shaped salary curves, and performance-based promotion, accepting the competitive ranking and Peter-Principle dynamics this entails [ch17 ¶5–6].

## Claims
- C1. Money's motivational meaning depends on need level: if the absolute size of a raise matters, the person is driven by physiological or safety needs; if the relative size matters, money is functioning as a measure of worth for a self-actualization-driven person [ch17 ¶3].
- C2. Because a middle manager cannot be paid by the piece and his work is woven into a team's, compensation can only approximate performance through compromise schemes such as a performance bonus [ch17 ¶5–6].
- C3. Bonus design must settle team-vs-individual linkage, the time period covered, and countable-vs-subjective measures; a workable answer splits a bonus across individual, team, and corporate performance factors, and the bonus share of total pay should rise with compensation level (roughly 50 percent for senior managers, 10–25 percent for middle managers) [ch17 ¶6–8].
- C4. Pure experience-only salary administration tells employees that performance does not matter much, while pure merit-only administration is impractical, so most companies run a compromise: common starting salaries with curves that diverge by individual performance [ch17 ¶11–12].
- C5. Any merit-based scheme requires competitive, comparative evaluation of individuals — someone must rank last — and this is a must despite being emotionally charged at work in a way it is not in sports [ch17 ¶13–14].
- C6. Promotions must be based on performance because they visibly communicate the organization's value system; the resulting Peter-Principle cycle, in which achievers alternate between meets-requirements and exceeds-requirements ratings until they plateau, has no acceptable alternative, since withholding challenge lets people atrophy [ch17 ¶15–20].
- C7. When someone is promoted over his head, management — which made the misjudgment — should recycle him into the job he previously did well and support him through the embarrassment, rather than force him out of the company [ch17 ¶21].

## Evidence
- E1. Teachers paid by seniority — case — supports C4 — school systems where evaluation is not tied even symbolically to pay show the experience-only message that performance is irrelevant [ch17 ¶11 "A good one gets paid the same salary as a bad one"] — caveat: Grove brackets the fairness question and speculates loosely about pass/fail grading.
- E2. Three-factor bonus scheme — thought-experiment — supports C3 — a worked example splits a manager's bonus into individual, team, and corporate components so each part is small but still spotlights performance [ch17 ¶8 "performance bonus is based on three factors"] — caveat: Grove concedes no scheme gives exactly what you want.
- E3. Last place in a race — analogy — supports C5 — Americans accept that a sports ranking must have a last finisher, showing the discomfort with workplace ranking is cultural, not logical [ch17 ¶14 "person who comes in last in a race feels comfortable"] — caveat: analogy assumes work and sport rankings are comparable.
- E4. Tracked promotion path (illustration) — thought-experiment — supports C6 — following one achiever through Jobs 1 and 2 shows the alternating ratings cycle, and holding a point-B performer back wastes capacity [ch17 ¶19 "not fully utilizing a human resource of the company"] — caveat: rests on Grove's stylized figure, not data.
- E5. The forced exit of the over-promoted — authority — supports C7 — Grove condemns the common rationalization for pushing out an over-promoted employee and reports recycled people later succeed at a second promotion [ch17 ¶21 "it is dead wrong to force someone"] — caveat: evidence is his personal experience only.

## Reader Assumptions
The reader is assumed to know Maslow's need hierarchy and the money-as-measure test from earlier in the book, the performance-appraisal difficulties of the preceding chapters, and the meets/exceeds rating jargon. Grove also assumes the reader is a supervisor with real discretion over raises, bonuses, and promotions, and shares a broadly American cultural frame about competition and the stigma of demotion.

## Foundational Vs Illustrative
Foundational: compensation as task-relevant feedback; the rising bonus percentage principle; the experience-vs-merit spectrum and its compromise; the necessity of competitive ranking; performance-based promotion and the reinterpreted Peter Principle; recycling. Illustrative: the Japanese-company and teacher examples, the three-factor bonus example, the race analogy, the promotion-path figure.

## Retain
Money and promotion are feedback instruments, not just rewards: tie a rising fraction of pay to performance, accept that merit means comparative ranking with a last place, promote on performance even at the cost of Peter-Principle plateaus, and recycle rather than expel those promoted too far.

## Tension
Grove insists compensation should obviously be tied to performance, yet in the same breath admits performance is very hard to assess precisely and that no bonus arrangement delivers exactly what you want — the chapter builds an edifice on a measurement it concedes is unreliable [ch17 ¶5]. Similarly, his recycling prescription runs against the social reality he himself documents, that demoted people are seen (and see themselves) as failures [ch17 ¶21].

## Recall
- Q: Why does Grove argue that the performance-bonus percentage should rise with a manager's total compensation, instead of being uniform across levels?
  A: Because incremental money loses material utility as compensation rises: a senior manager can absorb a 50 percent swing since the absolute dollars matter relatively little to him, while a middle manager still has real material needs, so large fluctuations would cause personal hardship. Limiting the middle manager's bonus to 10–25 percent still gives a taste of task-relevant feedback without endangering his livelihood [ch17 ¶4–6]. (difficulty: application)
- Q: According to Grove, what distinguishes the two types of meets-requirements performers, and why does he still endorse promoting people toward their level of incompetence?
  A: The noncompetitor has settled and faces no challenge to do more; the competitor is at meets level only because each time he exceeds requirements he is promoted into a harder job. Grove sees no alternative: refusing to promote an exceeds performer underuses the person, who atrophies back to meets level anyway, whereas promotion keeps him performing at ever more challenging levels [ch17 ¶19–20].
