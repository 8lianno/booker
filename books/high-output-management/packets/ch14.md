## Purpose
This chapter answers the question left open by the book's turn to motivation: given that a manager's most important responsibility is to elicit top performance from subordinates, which management style should he use? It supplies the book's contingency framework — task-relevant maturity — that later chapters on performance appraisal, promotion, and training presuppose when they ask how closely a manager should direct, support, or merely monitor a given person on a given task.

## Central Question
Is there a single best management style, and if not, what variable tells a manager which style is most effective in a particular situation?

## Thesis
No one management style is optimal in all conditions; the fundamental variable that determines the effective style is the subordinate's task-relevant maturity, so the manager must move between structured direction, two-way communication, and minimal monitoring as TRM varies — while always monitoring closely enough to avoid surprises [ch14 ¶4–9].

## Claims
- C1. Neither controlled behavioral-science research nor Intel's practice of rotating middle managers supports the existence of one best leadership style; high output is tied to particular combinations of certain managers and certain groups [ch14 ¶4–5].
- C2. The variable that determines the best style in a given situation is the subordinate's task-relevant maturity — a combination of achievement orientation, readiness to take responsibility, education, training, and experience — and it is specific to the task at hand, not a general trait [ch14 ¶6].
- C3. Effective style progresses with TRM: low TRM calls for structured, what-when-how direction; medium TRM for individual-oriented communication and support; high TRM for minimal involvement limited to agreeing on objectives — but at every level the manager must monitor closely enough to avoid surprises, the difference between delegating and abdicating [ch14 ¶9].
- C4. TRM is tied to a specific working environment: when the job's content, pace, or environment changes abruptly, an individual's or group's TRM drops and the manager must revert quickly to the structured what-when-how mode [ch14 ¶7–8].
- C5. As supervision moves from structured to communicating to monitoring, the degree of structure governing behavior does not diminish — it moves from externally imposed to internally given, which requires commonality of operational values that the supervisor is responsible for transmitting [ch14 ¶22–24].
- C6. Raising subordinates' TRM as rapidly as possible is high-leverage management: the high-TRM style takes less time, enables delegation, and ultimately hands motivation over to self-actualization, the most powerful source of energy a manager can harness [ch14 ¶26].
- C7. In practice managers misapply the framework: they avoid the middle style in favor of full immersion or total hands-off, they see themselves as far more communicating and delegating than their subordinates do, and they confuse social relationships with a communicating management style [ch14 ¶31–33].

## Evidence
- E1. Intel middle-manager rotations — data — supports C1 — when managers are rotated between similar groups, neither managers nor groups keep their high- or low-producing character, showing output comes from manager-group pairings rather than a superior style [ch14 ¶5 "high output is associated with particular combinations of certain managers"] — caveat: informal observation of Intel practice, not a controlled study.
- E2. Star sales manager moved into the plant — case — supports C4 — an extremely productive sales manager deteriorated when put in charge of a factory unit because the environment, content, and tasks were new; as his TRM rose he recovered, exposing the confusion of general competence with TRM [ch14 ¶7 "his task-relevant maturity in the new job was extremely low"] — caveat: single anecdote; Grove admits the outcome "should have been totally predictable."
- E3. Sergeant at the quiet army encampment — thought-experiment — supports C4 — a sergeant monitoring a high-TRM camp instantly switches to barking what-when-how orders when the enemy attacks, then eases off as fighting itself becomes routine and group TRM for the new task rises [ch14 ¶27 "reverts to a structured, task-oriented leadership style"] — caveat: hypothetical illustration, not an observed case.
- E4. Supervisor self-assessment test — data — supports C7 — Grove asked managers to assess their supervisors' styles and supervisors to assess their own; the supervisors overwhelmingly rated themselves as more communicating or delegating than their subordinates perceived [ch14 ¶32 "90 percent of the supervisors saw their style as more communicating"] — caveat: informal test with unreported sample size and method.
- E5. Parent-and-child development — analogy — supports C3 — parental style moves from "no" to accompanied bicycle rides to mere monitoring of the college-bound child as life-relevant maturity grows, mirroring the TRM progression of management styles [ch14 ¶11 "the most effective parental style changes"] — caveat: analogy imported from a different relationship; subordinates are not children.

## Reader Assumptions
Grove assumes the reader has accepted earlier arguments: that a manager's output is the output of his organization, that leverage and delegation-with-monitoring are the core tools, and that motivation (culminating in self-actualization) has just been explained — "Assuming we understand what motivates an employee" [ch14 ¶3]. He also assumes readers carry a bias he must argue against: that the "nice," communication-oriented style is morally better than giving orders [ch14 ¶10; ch14 ¶29].

Illustrative: the historical sketch of management fashion [ch14 ¶4], the sales-manager and freeway-driver examples [ch14 ¶7–8], the parent-child analogy [ch14 ¶11, ¶21], the sergeant story [ch14 ¶27], and the fishing-trip and friendship anecdotes [ch14 ¶33–34].

## Retain
There is no good or bad management style, only an effective or ineffective one for a given subordinate's task-relevant maturity in a given environment — so vary your style with TRM, work toward internalized structure through shared values, and never stop monitoring.

## Tension
The framework's own operating requirements are undermined by evidence Grove supplies: deciding a subordinate's TRM "is not easy," managers' personal preferences override the logically correct style even when they know the TRM is medium, and 90 percent of supervisors misjudge their own style [ch14 ¶31–32]. The chapter prescribes a diagnosis-driven method while showing that the diagnosticians are systematically biased, and it leaves the friendship question explicitly unresolved — "Everyone must decide for himself" [ch14 ¶34–35].

## Recall
- Q: A seasoned, highly competent sales manager is moved to run a factory unit of comparable size and scope, and his performance collapses. Using Grove's framework, explain why this "should have been totally predictable" and what his supervisor should have done differently.
  A: TRM is specific to the task and environment, not a general trait; the manager's personal maturity was unchanged but his task-relevant maturity in the new job was extremely low because its environment, content, and tasks were all new to him [ch14 ¶7]. Intel confused general competence with task-relevant maturity. His supervisor should have reverted to a structured, what-when-how style until the manager's TRM rose, then progressively shifted toward communication and finally minimal monitoring [ch14 ¶9]. (difficulty: application)
- Q: When a manager's style moves from structured direction to mere monitoring, what happens to the structure governing the subordinate's behavior, and what precondition makes this progression possible?
  A: The degree of structure does not really change — it moves from being externally imposed to being internally given, like the teenager who no longer needs to be told not to cross the highway [ch14 ¶22]. The precondition is commonality of operational values, priorities, and preferences, which the supervisor is responsible for transmitting; without shared values a supervisor cannot effectively delegate [ch14 ¶23–24].
