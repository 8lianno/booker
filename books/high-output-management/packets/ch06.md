## Purpose
This chapter converts the book's production-and-leverage machinery into the manager's daily working instrument: the meeting. It rehabilitates meetings against the management literature that treats them as waste, then supplies operating procedures for each meeting type. Later chapters presuppose it: peer-group decision-making (the next chapter, per ¶31), dual reporting (Chapter 9, per ¶31), and task-relevant maturity (introduced here at ¶13 and developed "later") all assume managers who can run and sit in meetings well.

## Central Question
If meetings consume so much managerial time, should managers fight them or master them — and what does mastering each kind of meeting concretely require?

## Thesis
Because a manager's two basic tasks — supplying information and know-how, and making or helping to make decisions — can only occur in face-to-face encounters, the meeting is nothing less than the medium through which managerial work is performed; the aim is therefore not to eliminate meetings but to make the time spent in them maximally efficient [ch06 ¶4].

## Claims
- C1. Meetings should not be fought as a curse of managerial existence: information transfer, know-how teaching, and decision-making happen only face-to-face, so the meeting is the medium of managerial work itself [ch06 ¶3–4].
- C2. The two basic managerial roles generate exactly two meeting types: regularly scheduled process-oriented meetings (knowledge shared, information exchanged) and ad hoc mission-oriented meetings designed to produce a specific output, frequently a decision [ch06 ¶5].
- C3. The one-on-one is the subordinate's meeting: its frequency is set by the subordinate's task-relevant maturity, and its agenda, outline, and tone are set by the subordinate, who prepares once while a supervisor with eight reports would otherwise prepare eight times [ch06 ¶13] [ch06 ¶17].
- C4. One-on-ones exert enormous leverage: roughly ninety minutes of a supervisor's time can enhance the quality of a subordinate's work for two weeks (eighty-plus hours), building the common information base that is the only route to efficient and effective delegation [ch06 ¶26].
- C5. In peer-group forums the supervisor's job is moderator, facilitator, and pace-controller — never lecturer — because pontificating is the surest way to undermine the free discussion that is the meeting's basic purpose [ch06 ¶36–37].
- C6. A mission-oriented meeting succeeds or fails on the chairman, whose main contribution occurs before the meeting begins: verifying the meeting is necessary at all, securing committed attendance of no more than eight people, enforcing punctuality as if lateness were theft, issuing a purpose-stating agenda, and sending prompt, specific minutes afterward [ch06 ¶47–53] [ch06 ¶83].
- C7. Grove inverts Drucker's warning: routine process-oriented meetings should absorb about 80 percent of problems, so the real sign of malorganization is people spending more than 25 percent of their time in ad hoc mission-oriented meetings [ch06 ¶84].

## Evidence
- E1. Grove's private lessons at young Intel — story — supports C1 — knowing little about memory devices or manufacturing, Grove had two subordinates tutor him by appointment, the pupil/supervisor taking notes; this teacher/subordinate arrangement became the enduring template for Intel one-on-ones, showing the meeting as the vehicle of know-how transfer [ch06 ¶11 "agreed to give me private lessons on memory design and manufacturing"] — caveat: a single-company origin story, offered as tone-setting rather than proof.
- E2. Sales-manager one-on-one on slowing orders — case — supports C4 — a subordinate's trend data persuaded Grove the slowdown was not seasonal; both left with congruent decisions (scale back growth; spread conservatism to other groups), so one meeting cascaded across the organization [ch06 ¶28 "the Intel sales manager affected all the other managers"] — caveat: leverage is asserted from one favorable episode, with no failure case examined.
- E3. Dollar cost of a meeting — data — supports C6 — at an estimated $100 per hour of manager time including overhead, ten managers for two hours equals a $2,000 expenditure that would normally require senior approval, yet can be committed "at a whim" [ch06 ¶49 "a meeting involving ten managers for two hours costs the company $2,000"] — caveat: the $100/hour figure is a rough estimate, not a measured cost.
- E4. Dinner-table analogy for staff meetings — analogy — supports C5 — because staff-meeting participants have long-established formal and informal standing with each other, decision-making there works like family conversation, whereas forums of relative strangers do not; hence the supervisor should moderate rather than dominate [ch06 ¶37 "like the dinner-table conversation of a family"] — caveat: analogy presumes a stable, long-tenured team.
- E5. Drucker on good time users — authority — supports C3 — Grove quotes Drucker to define the supervisor's one-on-one role as facilitating the subordinate's talk rather than airing his own problems, reinforcing that the meeting belongs to the subordinate [ch06 ¶19 "make the subordinates talk about theirs"] — caveat: none stated.

## Reader Assumptions
Grove assumes the reader has absorbed the earlier production framework — batching, set-up time, production control, interruptions, and managerial leverage — since the chapter repeatedly borrows those terms without re-deriving them (¶7, ¶22, ¶26). He also assumes a reader steeped in the anti-meeting orthodoxy of Drucker and Whyte (¶3), a conventional hierarchical firm with supervisors, direct reports, and multiple organizational levels, and a manager empowered to schedule, decline, or restructure meetings.

## Foundational Vs Illustrative
Foundational: the meeting-as-medium thesis (¶4), the process/mission taxonomy (¶5), one-on-one design rules and their leverage logic (¶13, ¶17, ¶26), the supervisor-as-moderator rule (¶36–37), the chairman's pre-meeting responsibilities and cost discipline (¶47–53), and the 80/20 reinterpretation of Drucker (¶84). Illustrative: the private-lessons origin story (¶11), the sales-manager episode (¶27–28), family one-on-ones with Grove's daughters (¶29), the Philippines plant agenda specimen (¶54–81), and the operating-room analogy for meeting discipline (¶82).

## Retain
A meeting is not overhead but the medium of managerial work: run process-oriented meetings (one-on-ones, staff meetings, operation reviews) with enough regularity and design that ad hoc decision meetings become rare — and judge your organization by how little of the 20-percent remainder it needs [ch06 ¶4] [ch06 ¶84].

## Tension
Grove opens by rebutting the school that damns meetings — explicitly including Drucker's rule that over 25 percent of time in meetings signals malorganization (¶3) — yet closes by conceding Drucker's exact threshold, merely re-aimed at ad hoc meetings, and by admitting a manager should "ideally... never have to call" a mission-oriented meeting [ch06 ¶84]. So the chapter simultaneously crowns meetings as the medium of managerial work and treats an entire class of them as a symptom of organizational failure; the boundary case — an organization heavy in scheduled meetings that merely relabels its ad hoc work — is left unexamined.

## Recall
- Q: Grove says the one-on-one "should be regarded as the subordinate's meeting." What structural reason does he give, and what does the subordinate's outline accomplish?
  A: The reason is preparation asymmetry: a supervisor with eight subordinates would have to prepare eight times, while each subordinate prepares only once, so the subordinate sets the agenda and tone. The outline forces the subordinate to think through every issue in advance, tells the supervisor at the outset what will be covered so he can pace the meeting by the "meatiness" of the items, and provides the framework for the supporting information the subordinate walks him through [ch06 ¶17]. (difficulty: recall)
- Q: A peer invites you to a two-hour decision meeting with eleven attendees and no stated purpose. Using this chapter, what should you do before accepting, and why?
  A: Apply the chairman's test even as an invitee: ask what the meeting is trying to accomplish and whether it is necessary, desirable, and justifiable — if the answers are not all yes, tell the person who invited you and push to get it called off early at a low-value-added stage, substituting a cheaper medium like a one-on-one, a call, or a note [ch06 ¶48–49]. The size alone is disqualifying: a decision meeting is hard to keep moving past six or seven people, and eight is the absolute cutoff, because decision-making is not a spectator sport [ch06 ¶51]. (difficulty: application)
