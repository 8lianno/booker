## Purpose
This chapter takes the book's earlier machinery — task-relevant feedback, managerial leverage, output vs. internal measures of the black box — and applies it to the single hardest interpersonal act a manager performs: judging a subordinate's performance and delivering that judgment face to face. It grounds the book's motivation chapters in a concrete institutional practice and sets up the following chapters on promotion/turnaround decisions, compensation as feedback, and training, all of which presuppose that the manager is willing to act as "judge and jury."

## Central Question
How should a manager assess a subordinate's performance and deliver that assessment so that the review actually improves future performance rather than merely recording the past?

## Thesis
The performance review is the most important form of task-relevant feedback a supervisor gives, and its one fundamental purpose is to improve the subordinate's performance — by raising skill and intensifying motivation; both halves of the job, assessing and delivering, demand deliberate technique because neither our culture nor our training prepares us for it [ch15 ¶11–14].

## Claims
- C1. The fundamental purpose of a performance review is to improve the subordinate's performance, working on two things: missing skills and intensified motivation; every other purpose (raises, discipline, direction) is secondary [ch15 ¶12].
- C2. The review is the single most important form of task-relevant feedback and one of a manager's highest-leverage activities, because it influences a subordinate's performance for a long time [ch15 ¶11].
- C3. Assessing a professional's performance cannot be strictly objective: the supervisor must weigh output measures against internal measures of the black box, weigh long-term against short-term contribution, and correct for the time offset between activity and the output it later produces [ch15 ¶18–24].
- C4. Managers must assess performance, not potential or form; a manager's rating cannot be higher than the rating of his organization, or the company signals that acting like a good manager beats performing like one [ch15 ¶26].
- C5. Delivering the assessment is governed by three L's — level with the subordinate, listen with all sensory capabilities until the message is truly received, and leave yourself (your anxieties and need for comfort) out of it [ch15 ¶30–36].
- C6. In a "blast" review the reviewer's job is to move the subordinate through the stages of problem-solving to assuming responsibility, and any outcome that includes a commitment to action is acceptable — agreement is desirable but not required [ch15 ¶58–62].
- C7. Review effort has its priorities reversed: supervisors lavish corrective detail on marginal performers while giving stars retrospective praise, yet concentrating on improving the stars is the high-leverage activity because they carry a disproportionate share of the work [ch15 ¶68–69].

## Evidence
- E1. The "distant stars" manager — case — supports C3 — a manager's organization posts a superb year, gets a superior review, then collapses the next year; Grove concludes the excellent output reflected work done years earlier and the rating was wrong, proving output indicators can lag the performance under review by about a year [ch15 ¶23 "the light from distant stars"] — caveat: single episode from Grove's own experience; the one-year offset is specific to that case.
- E2. The highly rated general manager of a money-losing unit — case — supports C4 — a supervisor rates a GM highly on knowledge and bearing while his business misses revenue month after month; Grove refuses to approve the review because rewarding form over results would teach the company to act like managers rather than perform [ch15 ¶26 "force yourself to assess performance, not potential"] — caveat: none stated.
- E3. The deadlocked review rewrite — story — supports C6 — Grove's subordinate agrees a review he wrote is superficial but refuses to spend time redoing it; Grove invokes his position ("I have to insist"), the subordinate resentfully commits, and the rewrite turns out well — commitment without agreement worked [ch15 ¶65 "he committed himself to redo the review"] — caveat: outcome quality rests on Grove's own report.
- E4. Middle managers analyzing their own reviews — data — supports C7 — a group of about twenty high-rated achievers examine reviews they received: well written but retrospective, showing supervisors justify high ratings instead of developing stars [ch15 ¶68 "made little or no attempt to define what the subordinate needed"] — caveat: small informal sample from one training exercise at Intel.
- E5. "Why do you insist on wanting to convince me?" — story — supports C6 — in one of Grove's first reviews he keeps pushing a subordinate to agree who has already promised to comply; the subordinate's question exposes that Grove's insistence served his own comfort, not the business [ch15 ¶63 "why do you insist on wanting to convince me"] — caveat: none stated.

## Reader Assumptions
Grove assumes the reader already holds the book's earlier apparatus: the managerial black box with measurable output, leverage as the test of where to spend managerial time, and task-relevant feedback as the driver of a self-actualized performer. He also assumes the reader is a practicing supervisor who both gives and receives reviews, works in a culture that avoids confrontation ("the well-mannered skirt potentially emotional issues"), and accepts that the organization legitimately mandates one person to judge another [ch15 ¶13–14].

## Foundational Vs Illustrative
Foundational: the purpose of the review as performance improvement (¶12); the assessment framework of output vs. internal measures, present value of long-term work, and time offset (¶20–24); assess performance not potential (¶26); the three L's (¶30–36); the stages of problem-solving and the "commitment to action is acceptable" standard (¶58–62); the reversed-priorities argument for reviewing aces (¶68–69). Illustrative: the middle-manager survey lists (¶4–9), the Hungarian "argument" anecdote (¶14), the tape-recorder and bad-professor images (¶32, ¶35), the worksheet example with its positives/negatives (¶41–53), and the mechanics of when to hand over the written review (¶74–75).

## Retain
A review exists for exactly one thing — improving the subordinate's future performance — so assess real output plus internal measures with judgment about timing, deliver it by leveling, listening totally, and leaving yourself out, and settle for commitment to action rather than agreement.

## Tension
Grove concedes the assessment is inherently subjective — the supervisor "walks a tightrope" between objectivity and judgment with no formula for weighting output against internal measures (50/50, 90/10, or 10/90) — yet he simultaneously demands the system's total integrity and refuses ratings that exceed the organization's results, leaving unresolved how a subordinate should trust a judgment the author admits cannot be objective [ch15 ¶18–20]. A second unresolved wrinkle: reviews should ideally hold no surprises, yet Grove admits the worksheet can startle even a diligent supervisor, who must then "swallow hard and bring it up" anyway [ch15 ¶54].

## Recall
- Q: Grove gave a manager a superior rating in a year when all his output measures were excellent, then called that rating "totally wrong." What did he miss, and what general assessment rule does the episode establish?
  A: He ignored internal measures — rising turnover and grumbling subordinates — because tangible output was outstanding; the next year the organization collapsed, revealing that the good output was "the light from distant stars," work done years earlier. The rule: a supervisor must look at the time offset between a subordinate's activity and the output it produces, and must have the judgment and courage to rate the activity of the year under review, trusting internal measures even against excellent output indicators [ch15 ¶22–23]. (difficulty: application)
- Q: In a blast review, the subordinate refuses to agree with your assessment but says he will do what you recommend. According to Grove, is this acceptable, and why?
  A: Yes — any outcome that includes a commitment to action is acceptable, because complex issues rarely yield universal agreement and you must assume a stated commitment is sincere. The manager is after the subordinate's performance, not his own psychological comfort; people need not side with you, only commit to the decided course [ch15 ¶62]. (difficulty: recall)
- Q: Why does Grove reject having a subordinate write a self-review as the input to his appraisal?
  A: Because reviewing a subordinate is a formal act of leadership: if the supervisor is prompted by the subordinate's own account, his judgment appears false and the subordinate feels cheated — a boss who must be told about your accomplishments plainly wasn't paying attention. The supervisor must commit to an up-front judgment to preserve the integrity and vitality of the review system [ch15 ¶72]. (difficulty: recall)
