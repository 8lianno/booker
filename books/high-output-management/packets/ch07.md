## Purpose
Extends the book's output framing to decision-making: a decision is itself an output of a managerial process, and in know-how businesses it must be produced by deliberately meshing knowledge power with position power. The chapter installs the ideal three-stage decision model and the six pre-decision questions, and it deepens the book's argument that the middle manager is the linchpin who links the two kinds of power. Later chapters on organizational form and control presuppose this divergence between position and knowledge and the disciplined process for bridging it.

## Central Question
How should decisions be made in a business where power based on knowledge diverges rapidly from power based on position?

## Thesis
Because the knowledge base of a know-how business changes rapidly, position power and knowledge power split apart, so good decisions require a structured group process — free discussion, a clear decision, and full support — worked out at the lowest competent level and framed in advance by six questions about what, when, and who [ch07 ¶4–6] [ch07 ¶8–11] [ch07 ¶29–30].

## Claims
- C1. In information- and know-how-based businesses, a rapid divergence develops between power based on position and power based on knowledge, because managers grow more technically obsolete as they rise; the faster the underlying know-how changes, the greater the divergence [ch07 ¶4–6].
- C2. The ideal decision-making process has three stages — free discussion in which all views are aired, a clear decision framed with utter clarity, and full support from everyone involved — and support does not require agreement, only honest commitment to back the decision [ch07 ¶8–11].
- C3. Decisions should be worked out and reached at the lowest competent level, by people closest to the situation who combine technical knowledge with judgment earned from experience; when one person lacks both, the group should mix knowledge-power and position-power people who speak as equals [ch07 ¶13–14].
- C4. The most common failure mode is the peer-group syndrome: fearing to stick their necks out, peers circle toward consensus instead of stating positions, and the remedy is the peer-plus-one approach plus the realization that nobody has ever died from a wrong business decision [ch07 ¶17–21].
- C5. It is legitimate — sometimes unavoidable — for the senior person to wield position-power authority once the clear-decision stage arrives without consensus, but destructive to wield it any earlier; timing the move from discussion to decision is itself a critical judgment [ch07 ¶27–28].
- C6. Settling six questions in advance — what decision, when, who decides, who is consulted, who ratifies or vetoes, who is informed — makes the decision output timely and prevents late vetoes from looking like political maneuvering, which must be avoided at all costs [ch07 ¶29–30] [ch07 ¶37].
- C7. When the final word differs dramatically from participants' expectations, the manager should announce it, adjourn, then reconvene to solicit views, giving people time to adjust and accept the unexpected [ch07 ¶38].

## Evidence
- E1. Fired auto executive — story — supports C2 — a news account of a manager told that people who succeed at his company "wait until they hear their superiors express their view" shows how suppressing free discussion produces bad decisions built on incomplete information [ch07 ¶8 "wait until they hear their superiors express their view"] — caveat: single anonymous news account of one company.
- E2. Intel role-playing session — case — supports C4 — at Intel's first management training session, a leaderless group of peers tackled a live issue and "did nothing but go around in circles" until the excluded chairman returned, intervened, and the problem was resolved quickly, giving the peer-plus-one approach its name [ch07 ¶17 "did nothing but go around in circles for some fifteen minutes"] — caveat: a staged demonstration with observers, not an ordinary working meeting.
- E3. Philippine plant siting decision — case — supports C6 — Grove walks the six questions through a real expansion decision: parallel managers from construction and manufacturing decided, Grove ratified after probing the group's thinking, and Gordon Moore was informed [ch07 ¶36 "the group decided to build next to our existing plant"] — caveat: an example where the process worked; Grove reports no failed application.
- E4. The "John Wayne" middle manager — story — supports C2 — a business-school hire frustrated by Intel's onerous group process quit for a company promising individual decision-making, then returned four months later, having realized that "if he could make decisions without consulting anybody, so could everybody else" [ch07 ¶39 "if he could make decisions without consulting anybody, so could everybody else"] — caveat: anecdote about one manager; also paired with an Alfred Sloan authority quote that group decisions "do not always come easily".

## Reader Assumptions
The reader has absorbed the book's production framing — that managerial work has measurable output — and the previous chapter's treatment of meetings, since the plant decision is ratified "at the meeting described on the agenda shown in the previous chapter" [ch07 ¶36]. Grove also assumes the reader comes from, or knows, the traditional model in which decision authority simply tracks position in the chain of command [ch07 ¶4], and that the reader accepts middle managers as the key operational layer [ch07 ¶6].

## Retain
Decisions are outputs: produce them through free discussion, a clear decision, and full support, at the lowest competent level, with the six questions (what, when, who decides, who consults, who ratifies/vetoes, who is informed) settled before the fact.

## Tension
The model demands that participants voice opinions "as equals," forgetting status differentials during free discussion [ch07 ¶13], yet its own rescue mechanism reinstates hierarchy: when peers stall, the senior person takes over even though "he is likely to be no more expert in the issues at hand than other members of the group—perhaps less" [ch07 ¶22]. Grove never fully resolves why a status-blind process should depend on a status-based godfather, nor how a manager reliably knows the "optimum point" at which prolonging free discussion turns from legitimate to destructive delay [ch07 ¶27–28].

## Recall
- Q: Why does Grove argue that a senior manager may legitimately impose a decision at one stage of the process but not another, and what makes the timing hard in practice?
  A: Position power may be wielded only when the clear-decision stage is reached and free discussion has failed to produce consensus — by then all views were aired without position-power prejudice, so the imposed decision still benefits from complete information. Using that authority earlier chills discussion and is destructive. Timing is hard because American managers find giving orders "not nice" and so prolong free discussion past the optimum point, while pushing prematurely forfeits the real issues that only surface after the superficial comments [ch07 ¶27–28]. (difficulty: application)
- Q: What is the peer-group syndrome, and what two remedies does the chapter offer?
  A: It is the tendency of a group of organizational equals to circle without deciding, because each member fears going against the group or sticking his neck out; positions get voiced only as a hedged group consensus. Remedies: the peer-plus-one approach, adding one more senior person to shape the meeting, and building self-confidence from the gut-level realization that nobody has ever died from a wrong business decision [ch07 ¶17–21]. (difficulty: recall)
