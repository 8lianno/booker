## Purpose
This closing section converts the entire book from reading material into a training program. It presupposes every major tool introduced earlier — limiting steps, inspections, indicators, leverage, one-on-ones, objectives and key results, task-relevant maturity, performance reviews — and assigns each one as a concrete, point-scored exercise the reader must actually perform. Nothing later depends on it; it exists so that the book's ideas survive contact with the reader's real job.

## Central Question
How does a reader turn the concepts of this book into changed managerial behavior — and how much practice is enough?

## Thesis
Reading the book is only the down payment: Grove leaves the reader a menu of point-scored assignments drawn from every part of the book and claims that honestly completing at least 100 points' worth will make the reader a distinctly better manager [ch19 ¶2–3].

## Claims
- C1. The book ends not with a summary but with homework: Grove asks the reader to do "something specific," leaving a set of assignments to choose from and perform honestly [ch19 ¶2].
- C2. Grove promises a specific payoff for a specific dose — completing at least 100 points' worth of the assignments will make the reader a distinctly better manager [ch19 ¶3].
- C3. The Production assignments apply the factory model to the reader's own work: classifying operations as process/assembly/test, locating the limiting step, placing inspections, and building paired quantity-and-quality indicators [ch19 ¶6–12].
- C4. The far larger Leverage section turns the book's people-management tools — work simplification, information gathering, tours, delegation monitoring, one-on-ones, objectives with key results, Maslow-based motivation, task-relevant maturity, and performance reviews — into scheduled practices with subordinates [ch19 ¶19–25] [ch19 ¶33–34] [ch19 ¶45–52].
- C5. The point values encode a hierarchy of importance: recurring, subordinate-facing institutions (installing indicator reviews in staff meetings, holding one-on-ones, cascading objectives, defining racetracks, evaluating and redoing reviews) are weighted 20 points, double the one-off self-analyses [ch19 ¶15] [ch19 ¶34] [ch19 ¶40–42] [ch19 ¶48].

## Evidence
- E1. Closing charge to the reader — authority — supports C1 — after noting the reader has invested money and about eight hours, Grove issues assignments rather than conclusions [ch19 ¶2 "I leave you with a set of assignments"] — caveat: he concedes the move risks sounding like a diet-book author.
- E2. The 100-point promise — authority — supports C2 — the payoff is stated as a conditional guarantee tied to a numeric dose of practice [ch19 ¶3 "you’ll be a distinctly better manager for it"] — caveat: the claim is asserted, not evidenced; compliance is self-graded.
- E3. Limiting-step assignment — case — supports C3 — a Production task transplants the breakfast-factory method onto the reader's own live project [ch19 ¶8 "identify the limiting step and map out the flow of work"] — caveat: worth only 10 points, one of the lighter items.
- E4. One-on-one assignment — case — supports C4 — a 20-point Leverage task, including briefing subordinates in advance and having them prepare, institutionalizes the practice [ch19 ¶33 "Hold a scheduled one-on-one with each of your subordinates"] — caveat: none stated.

## Reader Assumptions
Grove assumes the reader has just finished the whole book (roughly eight hours of reading), understands its vocabulary without re-explanation — limiting step, monitoring vs. gate-like inspections, leverage, mission- vs. process-oriented meetings, the six-question decision approach, Maslow hierarchy, task-relevant maturity — and manages real subordinates and projects on which the assignments can be performed.

## Foundational Vs Illustrative
Foundational: the assignment mechanism itself — the choose-your-own list, the point weights, and the 100-point completion threshold [ch19 ¶2–3]. Illustrative: each individual task is an application of a concept established earlier; none introduces new theory, and any subset summing to 100 points would satisfy the charge [ch19 ¶6–12] [ch19 ¶19–26] [ch19 ¶49–55].

## Retain
Do the work: pick assignments from the list and honestly complete at least 100 points' worth — favoring the 20-point items that install recurring practices (indicator reviews, one-on-ones, cascaded objectives, racetracks) — because that practice, not the reading, is what makes you a better manager [ch19 ¶3].

## Tension
The scheme rests entirely on the honor system and an unargued number: Grove himself admits he is "at the risk of sounding like the author of a diet book," asks only that readers perform the tasks "honestly," and offers no basis for why 100 points — rather than 60 or 200 — is the threshold at which one becomes distinctly better [ch19 ¶2–3]. Self-graded exercises like redoing one's own performance review have no external check on quality [ch19 ¶55].

## Recall
- Q: Why does Grove attach point values to the closing assignments instead of simply listing them?
  A: The points let him prescribe a dose rather than a syllabus: the reader may choose freely, but must accumulate at least 100 points to earn the promised improvement [ch19 ¶3]. The weighting also steers behavior — recurring, subordinate-facing institutions such as installing indicator reviews in staff meetings and holding scheduled one-on-ones carry 20 points, double the one-off analyses, nudging readers toward durable practices [ch19 ¶14–15] [ch19 ¶33–34]. (difficulty: application)
- Q: What are the two headings under which the assignments are grouped, and what does each cover?
  A: Production and Leverage. Production tasks apply the factory model to the reader's own work — process/assembly/test classification, the limiting step, inspections, and output indicators [ch19 ¶4] [ch19 ¶6–12]. Leverage tasks cover the managerial-leverage and people chapters: work simplification, tours, delegation, one-on-ones, objectives and key results, motivation, task-relevant maturity, and performance reviews [ch19 ¶18–25] [ch19 ¶45–52].
